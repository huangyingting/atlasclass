Chapter 5 · Analyzing functions

what the derivatives say

A derivative is a function’s slope. Read its sign and you know where the function rises and falls; read the sign of the second derivative and you know which way it bends.

Most of this chapter reads one function, stacked above its first and second derivatives:

f(x)=x3−6x2+9x+1f′(x)=3(x−1)(x−3)f′′(x)=6x−12\begin{aligned} f(x) &= x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 1 \\ f'(x) &= 3(x - 1)(x - 3) \\ f''(x) &= 6x - 12 \end{aligned}

Where f′f' is zero, ff is flat: a peak at (1, 5) and a valley at (3, 1). Where f′′f'' changes sign, ff changes how it bends: at (2, 3). The vertical lines join each feature to its cause.

The chapter ends with the payoff: the largest field a fence can hold, the largest box a sheet can fold into, and the highest point on a curve that isn’t a function.

5.1 · The Mean Value Theorem

somewhere, the instant matches the average

If ff is continuous on [a,b][a, b] and differentiable on (a,b)(a, b), then at some cc between, the tangent is parallel to the secant:

f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−af'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}

On [0,4][0, 4] the blue secant runs from (0, 1) to (4, 5), slope 5−14−0=1\tfrac{5 - 1}{4 - 0} = 1. Setting 3c2−12c+9=13c^2 - 12c + 9 = 1:

c=2±23≈0.845, 3.155c = 2 \pm \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} \approx 0.845,\ 3.155

Both lie inside, so here there are two. The theorem promises at least one; it does not say where.

Rolle’s theorem is the level case: if also f(a)=f(b)f(a) = f(b), some f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0. Here f(0)=f(3)=1f(0) = f(3) = 1, and f′(1)=0f'(1) = 0.

The conditions matter: ∣x∣|x| on [−1,1][-1, 1] has equal ends but a corner, and no flat tangent anywhere.

5.2 · Extreme values

global and local, and where to look

An absolute (global) maximum is the largest value on the whole domain in question. A relative (local) maximum is largest only among nearby values. Minimums alike.

The Extreme Value Theorem. A function continuous on a closed interval [a,b][a, b] has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there. Both conditions count: on all of the real line this ff has neither, since it runs off to ±∞\pm\infty.

Extremes can only happen at a critical point, an interior xx where f′(x)=0f'(x) = 0 or f′(x)f'(x) doesn’t exist, or at an endpoint. For our ff:

f′(x)=3(x−1)(x−3)f'(x) = 3(x - 1)(x - 3)

is zero at x=1x = 1 and x=3x = 3, the critical points. On [0.5,3.5][0.5, 3.5] the falling blue line touches the graph first at height 5 (the absolute max, at x=1x = 1) and last at 1 (the absolute min, at x=3x = 3). Each is also a relative extreme.

A critical point is only a candidate: f′f' can be zero without an extreme (5.4).

5.3 · Increasing and decreasing

the sign of f′

If f′(x)>0f'(x) > 0 on an interval, ff is increasing there; if f′(x)<0f'(x) < 0, decreasing. The tangent tilts up or down, and its slope is the height of f′f'.

To find the intervals, find where f′f' is zero or undefined, then test one point in each piece. f′f' can only change sign at those points.

intervaltestf′f'ff
x<1x < 1f′(0)=9f'(0) = 9+increasing
1<x<31 < x < 3f′(2)=−3f'(2) = -3−decreasing
x>3x > 3f′(4)=9f'(4) = 9+increasing

So ff increases on (−∞,1](-\infty, 1] and [3,∞)[3, \infty) and decreases on [1,3][1, 3]. Blue is up, red is down, above and below.

On the AP exam, justify with the sign: “ff is decreasing on (1,3)(1, 3) because f′(x)<0f'(x) < 0 there.”

5.4 · The first derivative test

does the sign change?

At a critical point cc, look at the sign of f′f' on either side. From + to −, ff has a relative maximum; from − to +, a relative minimum; no change, neither.

Take g(x)=x4−4x39g(x) = \tfrac{x^4 - 4x^3}{9}, so

5.5 · The candidates test

absolute extremes on a closed interval

On a closed interval the Extreme Value Theorem promises an absolute max and min, and they can only be at critical points or endpoints. So list those candidates, evaluate, and compare.

Take h(x)=x−2sin⁡xh(x) = x - 2\sin x on [0,π][0, \pi]. Then h′(x)=1−2cos⁡x=0h'(x) = 1 - 2\cos x = 0 when cos⁡x=12\cos x = \tfrac12, at x=π3x = \tfrac{\pi}{3}.

The absolute maximum is π\pi, at the endpoint x=πx = \pi, where h′h' isn’t zero at all. The absolute minimum is π3−3\tfrac{\pi}{3} - \sqrt3, at x=π3x = \tfrac{\pi}{3}. The bars are the three heights.

No sign chart is needed: only the values. Forgetting an endpoint is the classic mistake.

5.6 · Concavity

which way the graph bends

ff is concave up where f′f' is increasing: the slopes climb, and the graph lies above its tangents. It is concave down where f′f' is decreasing. So the sign of f′′f'' decides it.

f′′(x)=6x−12 {<0,x<2>0,x>2f''(x) = 6x - 12\ \begin{cases} < 0, & x < 2 \\ > 0, & x > 2 \end{cases}

so ff is concave down on (−∞,2)(-\infty, 2) and concave up on (2,∞)(2, \infty).

A point of inflection is where ff is continuous and the concavity changes: here (2,3)(2, 3). The shaded gap between the curve and its tangent flips sides as the point passes it.

f′′(c)=0f''(c) = 0 alone is not enough. For y=x4y = x^4, y′′(0)=0y''(0) = 0, yet y′′=12x2≥0y'' = 12x^2 \ge 0 on both sides: concave up throughout, no inflection point. As with extremes, look for a sign change.

5.7 · The second derivative test

cap or cup

Where f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0, the bend decides: if f′′(c)<0f''(c) < 0 the graph is a cap and f(c)f(c) is a relative maximum; if f′′(c)>0f''(c) > 0, a cup and a relative minimum.

f′′(1)=−6<0⇒maxf′′(3)=6>0⇒min\begin{aligned} f''(1) &= -6 < 0 &&\Rightarrow \text{max} \\ f''(3) &= 6 > 0 &&\Rightarrow \text{min} \end{aligned}

so f(1)=5f(1) = 5 is a relative maximum and f(3)=1f(3) = 1 a relative minimum.

The red parabola shares this point’s height, slope and second derivative. It opens down wherever f′′<0f'' < 0, up wherever f′′>0f'' > 0, and sits flat on top of the peak.

If f′′(c)=0f''(c) = 0 the test says nothing. For gg on page 5.4, g′′(0)=0g''(0) = 0 and x=0x = 0 was no extremum, but x4x^4 has y′′(0)=0y''(0) = 0 and a minimum. Fall back on the first derivative test.

If a continuous function has only one critical point on an interval, and it is a relative extreme, it is also the absolute one there. Optimization uses this.

5.8 · Sketching f, f′ and f″

one table, three graphs

Each graph is the slope of the one above it. Put their signs in one table and the shape of ff follows.

f′f'f′′f''ff
x<1x < 1+−rising, concave down
1<x<21 < x < 2−−falling, concave down
2<x<32 < x < 3−+falling, concave up
x>3x > 3++rising, concave up

Line up the features down the column:

  • zero of f′f' with a sign change ↔ extreme of ff
  • extreme of f′f' (zero of f′′f'' with a sign change) ↔ inflection point of ff
  • f′f' positive ↔ ff rising; f′f' rising ↔ ff concave up

Sketching f′f' from ff: mark where ff is flat (zeros), then where it is steepest (peaks and troughs of f′f').

5.9 · Reading f from f′

when all you have is the derivative

AP questions often give only the graph of the derivative. Everything about where pp rises and bends can be read from it; only pp’s height needs one value, here p(0)=1p(0) = 1.

p′(x)=14 x(x−3)2p'(x) = \tfrac14\,x(x - 3)^2
  • p′<0p' < 0 for x<0x < 0 and p′>0p' > 0 after, except p′(3)=0p'(3) = 0: pp decreases, then increases.
  • At x=0x = 0, p′p' crosses from − to +: a relative minimum.
  • At x=3x = 3, p′p' touches zero without crossing: no extremum.
  • p′p' increases on (−∞,1)(-\infty, 1) and (3,∞)(3, \infty): pp is concave up there, concave down on (1,3)(1, 3).
  • p′p' has a max at x=1x = 1 and a min at x=3x = 3: pp has inflection points at both.

So the flat point at x=3x = 3 is not a peak but an inflection point with a horizontal tangent. The red tangent on pp always has the slope that the red dot on p′p' shows.

5.10 · Introduction to optimization

one quantity to make largest

An optimization problem asks for the largest or smallest value of a quantity. Write it as a function of one variable, find that variable’s domain, and use the candidates test.

A farmer has 120 m of fence to enclose a rectangle along a river; the river side needs none. Call the two sides that meet the river xx. The side opposite the river gets the rest, 120−2x120 - 2x:

A(x)=x(120−2x)=120x−2x2A(x) = x(120 - 2x) = 120x - 2x^2

for 0≤x≤600 \le x \le 60. Then

A′(x)=120−4x=0 ⟹ x=30A'(x) = 120 - 4x = 0 \implies x = 30
xx (m)03060
AA (m²)018000

The largest field is 30 m by 60 m, 1800 m². Notice the side along the river is twice the others. The field and the dot on the graph move together.

5.11 · Solving optimization problems

the open box

A 30 cm square sheet has a square of side xx cut from each corner, and the sides fold up into an open box. Which xx holds the most?

The base is 30−2x30 - 2x on a side and the height is xx, so

V(x)=x(30−2x)2,0≤x≤15V(x) = x(30 - 2x)^2, \quad 0 \le x \le 15

By the product and chain rules,

V′(x)=(30−2x)2−4x(30−2x)=(30−2x)(30−6x)\begin{aligned} V'(x) &= (30 - 2x)^2 - 4x(30 - 2x) \\ &= (30 - 2x)(30 - 6x) \end{aligned}

V′=0V' = 0 at x=5x = 5 and x=15x = 15, an endpoint. The candidates:

xx (cm)0515
VV (cm³)020000

The largest box: 5 cm deep on a 20 cm square base, 2000 cm³. As a check, V′>0V' > 0 before x=5x = 5 and V′<0V' < 0 after, so it is the only relative maximum and therefore the absolute one.

Too shallow and the box is a tray; too deep and the base vanishes.

5.12 · Implicit relations

tangents and extremes without y = f(x)

A curve like x2−xy+y2=3x^2 - xy + y^2 = 3 is no function’s graph, but implicit differentiation still gives its slope, and the slope still finds its flat and vertical points.

2x−y−xy′+2yy′=02x - y - xy' + 2yy' = 0
dydx=y−2x2y−x\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y - 2x}{2y - x}

Horizontal tangents: the numerator is 0, so y=2xy = 2x. Substituting, x2−2x2+4x2=3x^2 - 2x^2 + 4x^2 = 3, so x=±1x = \pm 1: (1, 2) and (−1, −2).

Vertical tangents: the denominator is 0 (and the numerator isn’t), so x=2yx = 2y: y=±1y = \pm1, at (2, 1) and (−2, −1).

Is (1, 2) the highest point? Differentiate dydx\frac{dy}{dx} again by the quotient rule. At (1, 2) both y′y' and y−2xy - 2x are 0, so one term survives:

d2ydx2=(y′−2)(2y−x)(2y−x)2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{(y' - 2)(2y - x)}{(2y - x)^2}
=(0−2)(4−1)(4−1)2=−23= \frac{(0 - 2)(4 - 1)}{(4 - 1)^2} = -\tfrac{2}{3}

A cap: yy has a relative maximum of 2 there, the second derivative test, on a curve.
