Chapter 2 · Derivatives

the slope at a point

A derivative measures how fast a function is changing at a single input: the slope of its graph at one point.

The upper graph is f(x) = x³/3 − x. The red line touches it with the same steepness as the curve: the tangent line. The lower graph plots the tangent’s slope at every x. That is a new function, the derivative, f ′(x) = x² − 1.

Where f rises, f ′ is positive. Where f falls, f ′ is negative. At the peak and the valley, x = ±1, the tangent is flat and f ′ = 0.

This chapter defines the derivative as a limit, estimates it from data, finds where it fails to exist, and builds the rules that find it without a limit.

2.1 · Rates of change

from secant to tangent

A cart is at s(t) = t² metres after t seconds. How fast is it going at t = 1 s?

The average rate of change over [1, 1 + h] is the slope of the secant line through P and Q:

s(1+h)−s(1)h=(1+h)2−1h=2+h\begin{aligned}\frac{s(1+h) - s(1)}{h} &= \frac{(1+h)^2 - 1}{h} \\ &= 2 + h\end{aligned}
h (s)10.50.10.01
average velocity (m/s)32.52.12.01

As h → 0, Q slides into P, the secant turns into the tangent, and the averages close in on 2. The instantaneous rate of change at t = 1 is 2 m/s, the slope of the tangent at P.

A rate’s units are output units per input unit: metres per second here.

2.2 · The derivative

a limit of difference quotients

The derivative of f at x = a is the limit of the secant slopes, if that limit exists:

f′(a)=lim⁡h→0f(a+h)−f(a)h=lim⁡x→af(x)−f(a)x−a\begin{aligned}f'(a) &= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}\end{aligned}

The two forms are the same limit, with x = a + h. When it exists, f is differentiable at a.

Let a vary and f ′ is a function. For f(x) = x³/3 − x, expand and cancel the h:

f(x+h)−f(x)h=x2+xh+13h2−1 → x2−1\begin{aligned}&\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \\ &= x^2 + xh + \tfrac13h^2 - 1 \;\to\; x^2 - 1\end{aligned}

f ′(x), y ′, dy/dx and (d/dx) f(x) all name it. At x = 2, f ′(2) = 3, so the tangent line there is y=23+3(x−2)y = \tfrac{2}{3} + 3(x - 2).

2.3 · Estimating derivatives

from a table of values

From a table, a derivative can only be estimated, by the slope of a secant across the nearest readings. A cup of coffee cools; to estimate T ′(7), use the readings either side of t = 7:

t (min)035912
T(t) (°C)90.072.463.249.642.5
T′(7)≈T(9)−T(5)9−5=49.6−63.24=−3.40 ∘C/min\begin{aligned}T'(7) &\approx \frac{T(9) - T(5)}{9 - 5} \\ &= \frac{49.6 - 63.2}{4} \\ &= -3.40\ {}^\circ\text{C/min}\end{aligned}

In context: at t = 7 min the temperature is falling at about 3.4 °C per minute. Units: T’s over t’s.

In blue, the symmetric difference quotient [T(7 + h) − T(7 − h)]/2h shrinks onto the true tangent, slope −3.38.

2.4 · Differentiability and continuity

where the derivative fails

If f is differentiable at a, then f is continuous at a. The converse is false.

A derivative needs the secant slopes from the left (blue) and the right (red) to approach the same finite number. At x = 0:

leftright
corner, |x|−11
cusp, x2/3−∞+∞
vertical tangent, x1/3+∞+∞
jump−∞0.5

The first three are continuous at 0 but not differentiable there; the jump is not even continuous.

A join. g(x) = x² for x ≤ 1, 2x − 1 for x > 1. Both pieces give 1 at x = 1, so g is continuous; the slopes 2 and 2 agree, so g ′(1) = 2. Check continuity first: without it, matching slopes mean nothing.

2.5 · The power rule

bring the power down, lower it by one

For any real number n, ddx xn=n xn−1\dfrac{d}{dx}\,x^n = n\,x^{n-1}.

Why, for n = 3. Grow a cube of side x by Δx:

(x+Δx)3−x3Δx=3x2+3x Δx+(Δx)2 → 3x2\begin{aligned}&\frac{(x+\Delta x)^3 - x^3}{\Delta x} \\ &= 3x^2 + 3x\,\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2 \;\to\; 3x^2\end{aligned}

The three red slabs are the 3x²Δx; the blue rods and the black corner shrink away faster than Δx. At x = 2 the volume grows at 12 cubic units per unit of side.

2.6 · Sums and constant multiples

differentiate term by term

Derivatives pass through sums, differences and constant factors, and a constant alone has derivative 0:

(c)′=0(c f)′=c f′(f±g)′=f′±g′\begin{gathered}(c)' = 0 \qquad (c\,f)' = c\,f' \\ (f \pm g)' = f' \pm g'\end{gathered}

In the figure, u = x²/2 (blue) has slope x and v = 3 − x (grey) has slope −1. At every x the slope of u + v (black) is their sum, x − 1. The constant 3 lifts v without tilting it.

Example. Term by term:

f(x)=4x3−5x2+7x−2f′(x)=12x2−10x+7\begin{aligned}f(x) &= 4x^3 - 5x^2 + 7x - 2 \\ f'(x) &= 12x^2 - 10x + 7\end{aligned}

so f ′(1) = 9. The −2 contributes nothing.

Split first: (x² + 3)/x = x + 3x−1 has derivative 1 − 3/x².

2.7 · Sine, cosine, eˣ and ln x

four derivatives to know

With x in radians:

f(x)sin xcos xexln x
f ′(x)cos x−sin xex1/x

Top. The blue point plots the slope of sin x, and traces cos x: 1 at x = 0, 0 at the peaks, −1 at ±π.

Bottom. ex is its own derivative: its slope equals its height (dashed). ln x is its mirror image in y = x, so the slopes are reciprocals: ea at (a, ea), 1/ea at (ea, a).

Example. With the rules of 2.6:

ddx(3sin⁡x−2ex+5ln⁡x)=3cos⁡x−2ex+5x\begin{aligned}&\tfrac{d}{dx}\big(3\sin x - 2e^x + 5\ln x\big) \\ &= 3\cos x - 2e^x + \tfrac5x\end{aligned}

At x = 1: 3 cos 1 − 2e + 5 = 1.184.

2.8 · The product rule

not the product of the derivatives

(fg)′=f′g+fg′(f g)' = f'g + f g'

Think of f·g as the area of a rectangle with sides f and g.

Nudge x by Δx. The area gains a red strip f·Δg, a blue strip g·Δf, and a black corner Δf·Δg. Divide by Δx and let it go to 0: the strips give f g ′ + g f ′, and the corner vanishes.

From a table. f(2) = 3, f ′(2) = 2, g(2) = 2, g ′(2) = 1. For h = f g:

h′(2)=f′(2)g(2)+f(2)g′(2)=2⋅2+3⋅1=7\begin{aligned}h'(2) &= f'(2)g(2) + f(2)g'(2) \\ &= 2\cdot2 + 3\cdot1 = 7\end{aligned}

not f ′(2)g ′(2) = 2. That is the figure: sides 3 and 2, growing at 2 and 1 per unit of x.

From formulas. (x²ex)′ = 2x ex + x²ex = x ex(x + 2).

2.9 · The quotient rule

low d-high minus high d-low

(fg)′=f′g−fg′g2,g≠0\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - f g'}{g^2}, \quad g \ne 0

Order matters: swapping the two terms on top flips the sign.

Example. q(x) = (3x + 1)/(x² + 1): f = 3x + 1, f ′ = 3, g = x² + 1, g ′ = 2x.

q′(x)=3(x2+1)−(3x+1)(2x)(x2+1)2=−3x2−2x+3(x2+1)2\begin{aligned}q'(x) &= \frac{3(x^2+1) - (3x+1)(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2} \\ &= \frac{-3x^2 - 2x + 3}{(x^2+1)^2}\end{aligned}

At x = 1: q ′(1) = (3·2 − 4·2)/2² = −12-\tfrac{1}{2}. Dividing the derivatives, 3/(2·1) = 3/2, is wrong, even in sign.

The blue curve is q ′. It crosses zero where the tangent is flat: at the top (x = 0.721) and the bottom (x = −1.387).

2.10 · Tangent, cotangent, secant, cosecant

from sine, cosine and the quotient rule

Write each as a quotient of sine and cosine and use 2.9. For tan x = sin x/cos x, the top is cos x · cos x − sin x · (−sin x):

(tan⁡x)′=cos⁡2x+sin⁡2xcos⁡2x=1cos⁡2x=sec⁡2x\begin{aligned}(\tan x)' &= \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} \\ &= \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x\end{aligned}
f(x)tan xcot xsec xcsc x
f ′(x)sec² x−csc² xsec x tan x−csc x cot x

The “co-” functions take a minus sign.

In the figure, tan x climbs between its asymptotes at ±π/2 and is never flat: its slope sec² x (blue) is never below 1, and is 1 only at x = 0. At x = π/4 the slope is sec²(π/4) = 2.

Example. At x = π/3, (sec x)′ = sec(π/3) tan(π/3) = 2√3 ≈ 3.464.
