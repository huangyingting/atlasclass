Chapter 9 · Electric charge

counted in electrons

Atoms are built from charged parts: protons (+) in the nucleus, electrons (−) around it, and neutral neutrons. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.

particlechargemass
proton+e1.67 × 10−27 kg
electron−e9.11 × 10−31 kg
neutron0≈ mp
1 e=1.60×10−19 C1\ e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}

Equal numbers: neutral. Extra electrons: negative. Too few: positive. In charging solids only electrons move, and every charge is a whole multiple of e.

Conservation of charge: charge is never made or destroyed, only moved. Rub a rod with fur and one gets +q, the other −q.

9.1 · Charging objects

four ways to move electrons

Charging never makes charge. It moves electrons onto, off, or across an object.

frictionrub fur on PVC: electrons cross over
conductiontouch: spreads over a metal, stays put on an insulator
inductionno contact: push a metal’s electrons away, ground it
polarizationan insulator’s molecules stretch into dipoles

The −8 nC rod comes within 17 cm. The sphere’s free electrons crowd to the far side; grounding lets them escape. The sphere keeps +2.89 nC, opposite to the rod.

9.2 · Coulomb’s law

the inverse square

The force between two point charges grows with the product of the charges and falls with the square of the distance.

F=k∣q1q2∣r2F = \frac{k|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}

k=14πε0k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99 × 109 N·m²/C². Use sizes |q|; the signs only say attract or repel. The two forces are a third-law pair: equal, even though one charge is twice the other.

How strong? Give two 82 kg people 1 m apart 1% more protons than electrons: 3.93 × 107 C each, and F = 1.39 × 1025 N, enough to accelerate the Earth at 2.3 m/s².

9.3 · Net force

and the equilibrium point

With several charges, find each force on your charge, then add them as vectors: superposition.

A +0.1 μC test charge sits between A (+4 μC) and B (+1 μC), 45 cm apart. Both push it. The pushes balance where

kqAx2=kqB(d−x)2⇒x=30 cm\frac{kq_A}{x^2} = \frac{kq_B}{(d - x)^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 30\ \text{cm}

The balance point is always closer to the smaller charge. It need not lie between them: make B negative and it moves out past B, to 90 cm.

9.4 · The electric field

force per unit charge

A charge changes the space around it. A small positive test charge q0 placed anywhere feels a force; the field is that force per coulomb.

E⃗=F⃗q0=kQr2 r^\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0} = \frac{kQ}{r^2}\,\hat{r}

r̂ points away from Q. For +Q the field points out; for −Q, in. Units N/C.

field linesdirection, and strength by how closely they crowd; start on +, end on −
field vectorssize too: 899, 225, 100 N/C at 10, 20, 30 cm

Each charge drawn alone, 10× full size; E 10 cm per 100 N/C. The +0.1 nC test charge’s force is drawn as long as E. Click for r̂.

9.5 · The electric dipole

fields add as vectors

Two opposite charges ±q, 2a apart. E at P is the vector sum of each charge’s field.

On the axis both fields lie along the line and the nearer charge wins:

E=kq(x−a)2−kq(x+a)2=4kqax(x2−a2)2E = \frac{kq}{(x-a)^2} - \frac{kq}{(x+a)^2} = \frac{4kqax}{(x^2-a^2)^2}

On the bisector the y parts cancel and the x parts add:

E=2⋅kqa2+y2⋅cos⁡θ=2kqa(a2+y2)3/2E = 2 \cdot \frac{kq}{a^2+y^2} \cdot \cos\theta = \frac{2kqa}{(a^2+y^2)^{3/2}}

Far away these become 4kqa/x34kqa/x^3 and 2kqa/y32kqa/y^3: the two fields nearly cancel, so E falls as 1/r31/r^3.

9.6 · A charged rod

add up the slices

Cut the rod into slices dx, each holding dq = λ dx (λ=Q/L\lambda = Q/L = 10 nC/m). Each slice is a point charge; integrate.

On the axis, a past the end:

E=∫aa+Lkλ dxx2=kλ[1a−1a+L]=kQa(a+L)E = \int_a^{a+L} \frac{k\lambda\,dx}{x^2} = k\lambda\left[\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{a+L}\right] = \frac{kQ}{a(a+L)}

On the bisector, a above the middle, the x parts cancel:

E=∫kλa dx(x2+a2)3/2=kQaL2/4+a2E = \int \frac{k\lambda a\,dx}{(x^2+a^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{kQ}{a\sqrt{L^2/4 + a^2}}

Far away (a ≫ L) both become kQ/a2kQ/a^2, a point charge. Close to the middle (a ≪ L) it is 2kλ/a2k\lambda/a.

9.7 · A ring of charge

and a disc

Every piece of a ring is the same distance from a point P on its axis. The sideways parts cancel; only cos⁡φ=z/z2+R2\cos\varphi = z/\sqrt{z^2+R^2} of each survives.

E=∫02πkλR dθ⋅z(z2+R2)3/2=kQz(z2+R2)3/2E = \int_0^{2\pi} k\lambda R\,d\theta \cdot \frac{z}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{kQz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}

Zero at the centre, kQ/z2kQ/z^2 far away, and a peak between, at z=R/2z = R/\sqrt{2} = 14.1 cm.

A disc is a stack of rings, dq = σ 2πr dr:

E=σ2ε0(1−zz2+R2)E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\right)

Just above its centre it is σ/2ε0\sigma/2\varepsilon_0, not zero: the disc has charge right under P.

9.8 · A uniform field

projectile motion, again

Between two large, oppositely charged plates the field is the same everywhere, from + to −. A charge there feels a constant force and moves like a thrown ball.

F=qE=maF = qE = ma

An electron enters at 2.0 × 107 m/s; E = 5000 N/C. Its force points against E, toward the + plate.
