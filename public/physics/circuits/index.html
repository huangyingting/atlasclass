Chapter 13 · Current

charge on the move

A battery holds two terminals at a fixed potential difference. Join them with a wire and a field inside the metal pushes its free charges along: a current.

I=dqdt=nqAvd,J=IA=nqvd=σEI = \frac{dq}{dt} = nqAv_d,\quad J = \frac{I}{A} = nqv_d = \sigma E

1 A = 1 C/s, counted as if positive charge moved; electrons drift the other way. A slab Δx = vdΔt long holds nqAΔx, all of which crosses A in Δt. In general I=∫J⃗⋅dA⃗I = \int \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{A}.

1 A in 1 mm² of copper: n8.5 × 1028 m⁻³
drift speed vd0.074 mm/s

Each electron speeds up, hits an ion, stops, starts again (the Drude model), averaging vd: 3.8 h per metre. A lamp still lights at once, because the field races down the wire.

13.1 · Resistance

long and thin resists more

Resistance R=ΔVIR = \frac{\Delta V}{I}, in ohms (V/A), is how hard it is to push a current through something.

R=ρLA=LσAR = \frac{\rho L}{A} = \frac{L}{\sigma A}

The material sets the resistivity ρ=1σ\rho = \frac{1}{\sigma} (copper 1.68 × 10−8, nichrome 1.10 × 10−6 Ω·m); the shape sets L/A. Twice as long: two pieces in series, twice R. Twice the diameter: four strands side by side, a quarter of R.

Nichrome wires 0.5 and 1.0 mm across, 1.5 V on each. Carriers drift equally fast in both (same E=ΔVLE = \frac{\Delta V}{L}); the thick one has four times as many.

13.2 · Ohm’s law

V = IR

For an ohmic conductor the current is proportional to the voltage across it; the ratio is its resistance.

Start from Ohm’s law in the metal, J = σE, with E=ΔVLE = \frac{\Delta V}{L} along a wire:

I=JA=σA ΔVL⇒ΔV=IRI = JA = \frac{\sigma A\,\Delta V}{L}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta V = IR

6 V across 3 Ω drives 2.0 A. Each point is drawn at the height of its potential: the battery lifts charge 6 V, ideal wires stay level, and the resistor is a ramp down. Following the current through a resistor, you lose potential.

13.3 · Power

P = IΔV

Each coulomb gains ΔV joules in the battery and gives them up in the resistor as heat and, in a bulb, light.

P=dUdt=I ΔV=I2R=ΔV2RP = \frac{dU}{dt} = I\,\Delta V = I^2R = \frac{\Delta V^2}{R}

The last two use ΔV = IR: I²R when you know the current (series), ΔV2R\frac{\Delta V^2}{R} when you know the voltage (parallel). Unit: the watt, J/s.

IΔVI\Delta V0.50 A×12 V=6.0 W0.50\ \text{A} \times 12\ \text{V} = 6.0\ \text{W}
I2RI^2R(0.50 A)2×24 Ω=6.0 W(0.50\ \text{A})^2 \times 24\ \Omega = 6.0\ \text{W}
ΔV2/R\Delta V^2/R(12 V)2/24 Ω=6.0 W(12\ \text{V})^2 / 24\ \Omega = 6.0\ \text{W}

13.4 · Emf

a real battery

The emf ℰ is the energy a battery’s chemistry gives each coulomb. Not a force, despite the name.

A real battery has an internal resistance r, and the current loses Ir inside it:

ΔVterminal=E−Ir,I=ER+r\Delta V_\text{terminal} = \mathcal{E} - Ir,\quad I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r}

Here ℰ = 12 V, r = 2 Ω, and the bulb’s R sweeps 0.5–8 Ω. More current, lower terminal voltage, and I²r heats the battery. The bulb gets E2R(R+r)2\frac{\mathcal{E}^2 R}{(R + r)^2}; dPdR=0\frac{dP}{dR} = 0 at R = r, where it is brightest: E24r=18 W\frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{4r} = 18\ \text{W}.

13.5 · Series and parallel

one path, or side by side

In series the current has one path, so it is the same through each resistor and the voltages add. In parallel every branch spans the same two points, so the voltage is the same and the currents add.

Rs=R1+R2,1Rp=1R1+1R2+⋯R_s = R_1 + R_2,\quad \frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \cdots

13.6 · Combinations

reduce, then work back

A circuit of series and parallel pieces reduces, step by step, to one equivalent resistor. Then work backwards for each voltage and current.

13.7 · Kirchhoff’s rules

charge and energy, kept

Two batteries in different branches don’t reduce to one resistor. Kirchhoff’s two rules always work.

  • Junction rule (charge is conserved): in = out. At A, I1 = I2 + I3.
  • Loop rule (energy is conserved): ∑ΔV=0\sum \Delta V = 0 round any loop. With the current through a resistor, −IR; through a battery − to +, +ℰ.
1:−10I1−5I2−10I1+12=0\text{1:}\quad -10I_1 - 5I_2 - 10I_1 + 12 = 0
2:−20I3+6−20I3+5I2=0\text{2:}\quad -20I_3 + 6 - 20I_3 + 5I_2 = 0

So I1 = 0.52 A, I2 = 0.33 A, I3 = 0.19 A, the organizer’s answers. They need the 6 V battery’s + end at the bottom, as drawn here. Its sheet draws + on top: then loop 2 has −6 and I3 = −0.082 A, current the other way.

13.8 · RC circuits

charging and discharging

At a the battery charges the capacitor through R; at b the capacitor pushes the current back through R until it is empty.

E−IR−qC=0,I=dqdt\mathcal{E} - IR - \frac{q}{C} = 0,\quad I = \frac{dq}{dt}
q=EC(1−e−t/RC)q = \mathcal{E}C\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)

(Separate: ∫dqEC−q=∫dtRC\int \frac{dq}{\mathcal{E}C - q} = \int \frac{dt}{RC}.) I=ERe−t/RCI = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}e^{-t/RC}. τ = RC = 2.0 s: after one τ, q is 63.2% of ℰC = 1.2 mC and I 36.8% of ℰ/R = 0.6 mA. Discharging, q = Qmaxe−t/RC.

Empty, a capacitor acts as a wire; full, as a break. Of the battery’s Qℰ = 14.4 mJ, half is stored, half is heat.
