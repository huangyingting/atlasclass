Momentum and Impulse

Chapter 5 · Momentum

how hard it is to stop

Momentum measures how hard a moving object is to stop: its mass times its velocity, a vector in kg·m/s.

p=mvp = mv

A light, fast ball and a heavy, slow cart can carry the same momentum:

massvelocitymomentumKE
ball0.5 kg4.0 m/s2.0 kg·m/s4.0 J
cart2.0 kg1.0 m/s2.0 kg·m/s1.0 J

Not the same kinetic energy, though: K=p22mK = \frac{p^2}{2m}, so the lighter one carries more. A change in momentum is Δp = mΔv.

5.1 · Impulse

a force over a time

Push on an object for a while and its momentum changes. The push times the time is the impulse.

J=FΔt=ΔpJ = F\Delta t = \Delta p

The 0.5 kg ball arrives at +3.0 m/s and leaves at −3.0 m/s: Δp = −3.0 kg·m/s, twice its momentum, since it must stop and come back.

The wall’s push rises and falls over 0.10 s, peaking at 47 N. The area under the force–time graph is the impulse, 3.0 N·s: the same as a steady 30 N for 0.10 s. It points the way the force does, away from the wall.

AP Physics C: for a changing force the impulse is the integral J=∫F dtJ = \int F\,dt. For this pulse:

∫0τFpksin⁡(πtτ)dt=2Fpkτπ=3.0 N⋅s\int_0^\tau F_\text{pk}\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{\tau}\right) dt = \frac{2F_\text{pk}\tau}{\pi} = 3.0\ \text{N}\cdot\text{s}

5.2 · Less force

the same Δp, over more time

Two eggs fall 32 cm and land at 2.5 m/s. Each must lose 0.15 kg·m/s, so each needs the same impulse.

The pillow gives way and takes 0.18 s; the floor takes 0.03 s. FΔt is fixed, so six times less time means six times the force:

stopping timeaverage net forcepeak
pillow0.18 s0.83 N1.31 N
floor0.03 s5.00 N7.85 N

Airbags, crumple zones and bent knees can’t change Δp; they stretch Δt.

5.3 · Conservation

carts that stick

Treat both carts as one closed system, with no net outside force. Its total momentum can’t change.

The carts give each other equal and opposite impulses: whatever one loses, the other gains.

m1v1+m2v2=(m1+m2)v′m_1v_1 + m_2v_2 = (m_1 + m_2)v'

2.0 kg at 3.0 m/s meets 1.0 kg at −1.5 m/s. They stick at v′=4.53.0=1.5 m/sv' = \frac{4.5}{3.0} = 1.5\ \text{m/s}. Perfectly inelastic: KE is lost to heat, sound and dents.

5.4 · Elastic collisions

carts that bounce

If the carts bounce apart and no energy is lost, the collision is elastic: momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.

1.0 kg at 4.0 m/s hits 3.0 kg at rest. Two unknowns, two equations:

m1v1+m2v2=m1v1′+m2v2′m_1v_1 + m_2v_2 = m_1v_1' + m_2v_2'Δp=0\Delta p = 0
12m1v12+12m2v22=12m1v1′2+12m2v2′2\tfrac12 m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2^2 = \tfrac12 m_1v_1'^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2'^2ΔKE=0\Delta KE = 0

So v₁′ = −2.0 m/s and v₂′ = 2.0 m/s. Equal and opposite 6.0 N·s impulses: the smaller mass gets the bigger Δv.

5.5 · Explosions

pushed apart from rest

Two carts sit together at rest; a spring between them is released. The total momentum was zero, so it stays zero.

(m1+m2)v=m1v1′+m2v2′(m_1 + m_2)v = m_1v_1' + m_2v_2'

With v = 0 the two momenta are equal and opposite, so the 1.0 kg cart moves three times as fast as the 3.0 kg one. The spring’s 6.0 J becomes kinetic energy: KE increases.

5.6 · Ballistic pendulum

momentum, then energy

A 0.2 kg lump of clay at 10 m/s sticks in a 1.0 kg block on a 1.2 m string, and the block swings up. Two steps, two laws:

steplawhere
the hitΔp=0\Delta p = 0: mv=(m+M)v′mv = (m+M)v'v′ = 1.67 m/s
the swingΔE=0\Delta E = 0: 12v′2=gΔy\tfrac12 v'^2 = g\Delta yΔy = 14.2 cm

Energy isn’t kept across the hit (10.0 J → 1.67 J), and momentum isn’t kept across the swing (the string and gravity are outside forces). With Δy = L(1 − cos θ), θ = 28.1°:

5.8 · Centre of mass

it flies on as if nothing happened

The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a system. Internal pushes can’t change how it moves: on the cart pages it glided straight through every collision.

xcm=m1x1+m2x2m1+m2x_\text{cm} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2}{m_1 + m_2}

A 1.0 kg shell is fired at 10 m/s, 60° up, and bursts into equal halves at the top. The rear half is left at rest and drops to R2\frac{R}{2}. The front half takes all the momentum, v2′=m1+m2m2 vx=10.0 m/sv_2' = \frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_2}\,v_x = 10.0\ \text{m/s}, and lands at 3R2\frac{3R}{2}. The centre of mass lands at R = 8.84 m, as if it never burst.

Tip: the two pieces always get equal and opposite impulses, so the lighter one gets the bigger Δv.

5.11 · AP Physics C · Parts and holes

add the pieces, subtract the gaps

For a body made of simple pieces, put each piece’s mass at its own centre of mass and add. A hole is a piece of negative mass.

xcm=mAxA+mBxBmA+mBx_\text{cm} = \frac{m_A x_A + m_B x_B}{m_A + m_B}

That point is in the gap, not on the L. Like a ring or a boomerang, it can’t balance on a pin, but hung by a string it turns until its centre of mass is straight below the knot.
