Circular Motion

Chapter 3 · Circular motion

steady speed, still accelerating

A ball whirled round on a string keeps the same speed, yet it is accelerating the whole time. Its velocity changes direction at every point on the circle.

Velocity is a vector: it changes if its size or its direction changes. This ball circles a smooth table at 2.0 m/s on a 0.80 m string; the faint images, every ⅛ lap, are evenly spaced.

3.1 · Centripetal force

a job, not a new force

Acceleration toward the centre needs a net force toward the centre: ΣF=mac=mv2r\Sigma F = ma_\text{c} = \frac{mv^2}{r}. That is not a new kind of force, just whichever real force points inward:

ball on a stringtension T
water in a swung pailnormal force from the pail
car on a flat curvestatic friction fs
car on a banked curvepart of the normal force
Moon round the Earthgravity Fg

Here FN = mg, and T=mv2rT = \frac{mv^2}{r}: 1.00 N at 2.0 m/s, 4.00 N at 4.0 m/s. Double the speed, four times the tension; halve the radius, double it.

3.2 · A car on a curve

friction does the turning

On a flat curve the only sideways force on the car is the road’s static friction, toward the centre. Static, because the tyres don’t slide sideways; it can be at most μsFN.

ΣFy=0\Sigma F_y = 0FN=mg=11.76 kNF_\text{N} = mg = 11.76\ \text{kN}
ΣFx=mac\Sigma F_x = ma_\text{c}fs=mv2/r=5.88 of 9.41 kNf_\text{s} = mv^2/r = 5.88\ \text{of}\ 9.41\ \text{kN}

μsmg=mv2r\mu_\text{s} mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}; the mass cancels:

vmax=μsgr=8.85 m/sv_\text{max} = \sqrt{\mu_\text{s} gr} = 8.85\ \text{m/s}

Faster than that, the car slides off along the tangent: straight on, not flung outward.

3.3 · Swung in a cone

the string tilts

Whirl a ball on a string and the string can’t stay level: something must hold the ball up against gravity. The string tilts down until its pull does both jobs.

Measure θ from the vertical. Then the horizontal part uses sin θ and the vertical part uses cos θ:

ΣFy=0\Sigma F_y = 0Tcos⁡θ=mgT\cos\theta = mg, so T=mg/cos⁡θ=2.26 NT = mg/\cos\theta = 2.26\ \text{N}
ΣFx=mac\Sigma F_x = ma_\text{c}Tsin⁡θ=mv2/r=1.13 NT\sin\theta = mv^2/r = 1.13\ \text{N}

Divide one by the other and the tension cancels:

gtan⁡θ=v2r=acg\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{r} = a_\text{c}

Here a 1.0 m string at θ = 30° traces a circle of r = 0.50 m, so the ball must go 1.68 m/s, once round every 1.87 s. A perfectly level string is impossible: nothing would balance mg.

3.4 · Vertical circles

top and bottom of a loop

In a vertical loop gravity joins in. At the bottom and top the centre is straight up or down, so there the net force is vertical: mv2r\frac{mv^2}{r}.

bottom: FN−mg=mv2/rF_\text{N} - mg = mv^2/rFN=m(v2/r+g)=32.8 kNF_\text{N} = m(v^2/r + g) = 32.8\ \text{kN}
top: FN+mg=mv2/rF_\text{N} + mg = mv^2/rFN=m(v2/r−g)=3.4 kNF_\text{N} = m(v^2/r - g) = 3.4\ \text{kN}
FN top=0:vmin=gr=7.67 m/sF_\text{N top} = 0: \quad v_\text{min} = \sqrt{gr} = 7.67\ \text{m/s}

Same for a pail of water swung overhead. This 500 kg cart tops the loop at 10.0 m/s and reaches 18.3 m/s at the bottom; in between, part of the net force speeds it up or slows it down.

3.5 · Banked curves

tilt the road

Tilt the road and the normal force tilts with it, by the bank angle θ. Its horizontal part turns the car, with no friction at all.

ΣFy=0\Sigma F_y = 0FNcos⁡θ=mg, FN=12.51 kNF_\text{N}\cos\theta = mg,\ F_\text{N} = 12.51\ \text{kN}
ΣFx=mac\Sigma F_x = ma_\text{c}FNsin⁡θ=mv2/r=4.28 kNF_\text{N}\sin\theta = mv^2/r = 4.28\ \text{kN}
tan⁡θ=v2rg\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}

Each bank has one proper speed, rgtan⁡θ\sqrt{rg\tan\theta}: 5.97 m/s here. Faster, the car drifts up the bank; slower, it slides down.

3.6 · Angular velocity

v = rω

Every point on a spinning disc turns through the same angle in the same time: one angular velocity ω. Points farther out cover more distance, so they move faster.

ω=Δθ/t\omega = \Delta\theta/trad/s (or rpm)
s=rΔθs = r\Delta\thetaarc length; 1 rad when s=rs = r
v=rωv = r\omegatangential speed; T=2π/ωT = 2\pi/\omega

At ω = 2.50 rad/s (23.9 rpm; rpm×2π60=rad/s\text{rpm} \times \frac{2\pi}{60} = \text{rad/s}) the block at 0.40 m moves at 1.00 m/s, the one twice as far out at 2.00 m/s. Both take 2.51 s per turn; mass doesn’t matter.

3.7 · Gravitation

every mass pulls every other

Every mass attracts every other with a force proportional to both masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.

Fg=Gm1m2r2F_\text{g} = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}

G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². These spheres pull with 133 μN at 1.0 m (the weight of half a grain of rice); three times as far, a ninth: 14.8 μN. The Earth gives g=GMERE2=9.80 m/s2g = \frac{GM_\text{E}}{R_\text{E}^2} = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2: 784 N on an 80 kg person, just mg.

3.8 · Orbits

gravity on a string

A satellite in a circular orbit is a ball on a string, with gravity as the string. For any problem in space, start from Fg = Fc:

GMmr2=mv2r→v=GMr\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}

The satellite’s own mass cancels. Put in v=2πrTv = \frac{2\pi r}{T} and out comes Kepler’s third law, T² ∝ r³:

T2=(4π2GM)r3T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\right) r^3
orbit radiusvTa
2 RE = 12760 km5.59 km/s3.98 h2.45 m/s²
4 RE = 25520 km3.95 km/s11.27 h0.61 m/s²

Higher orbit: longer period, slower speed. That is the opposite of the disc, where every point shares one ω and the outside moves fastest. Real orbits are ellipses with the planet at one focus (Kepler’s first law), sweeping equal areas in equal times (his second). The next page draws one.

3.9 · Elliptical orbits

equal areas in equal times

Most orbits are ellipses, with the Earth at one focus (Kepler’s first law). Gravity has no torque about the Earth’s centre, so angular momentum mvr⊥ is conserved. At perigee and apogee v ⟂ r:

rpvp=ravar_\text{p} v_\text{p} = r_\text{a} v_\text{a}
perigee, 2 REvp = 6.85 km/s
apogee, 6 REva = 2.28 km/s

Three times as far, a third as fast. The radius sweeps equal areas in equal times (Kepler’s second law); his third holds with the semi-major axis a for r.

3.10 · Energy on an orbit

kinetic and potential trade

Only gravity acts, and gravity is conservative, so the satellite–Earth system keeps its mechanical energy:

12mv2−GMmr=E,constant\tfrac12 mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = E, \quad \text{constant}

Falling toward perigee, gravity has a part along v. It does positive work: U=−GMmrU = -\frac{GMm}{r} drops and K grows by as much. Climbing out, the same part slows it.

3.11 · AP Physics C · Potential energy

U = −GMm/r, zero at infinity

Far from the ground g changes and mgh fails. Set U = 0 at infinity; integrate Fr=−GMmr2F_r = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}:

U=−∫∞rFr dr′=GMm[−1r′]∞r=−GMmrU = -\int_\infty^r F_r\,dr' = GMm\left[-\frac{1}{r'}\right]_\infty^r = -\frac{GMm}{r}

Negative, because gravity does positive work pulling m in from infinity. Only differences matter, and −dUdr-\frac{dU}{dr} gives Fr back. Near the ground, r = R + h with h ≪ R:

ΔU≈(GMmR2)h=mgh\Delta U \approx \left(\frac{GMm}{R^2}\right) h = mgh

mgh is 1.6% high at 100 km; click the curve. 1 m per 4000 km across, 10 cm per GJ down, 20 cm per kN.

3.12 · AP Physics C · Escape

bound below zero

In a circular orbit mv2=GMmrmv^2 = \frac{GMm}{r}, so K=−U2K = -\frac{U}{2} and

E=K+U=−GMm2rE = K + U = -\frac{GMm}{2r}

Raising 1000 kg from 2 to 4 RE costs 7.8 GJ, yet it ends up slower: U gains 15.6 GJ, K loses 7.8. To reach r → ∞ at all takes E ≥ 0:

12mv2=GMmR→vesc=2GMR\tfrac12 mv^2 = \frac{GMm}{R} \quad\rightarrow\quad v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}

11.2 km/s from the ground, in any direction; 7.91 km/s = 2 vc\sqrt{2}\,v_\text{c} from 2 RE. Below it, an ellipse; at it, a parabola; above, a hyperbola.
