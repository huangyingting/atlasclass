Chapter 6 · Integration

adding up the pieces

A derivative takes a changing quantity apart into its rate. An integral puts it back together: it adds up rate × width, slice by slice, into a total.

The figure stands rectangles under y=1+x24y = 1 + \tfrac{x^2}{4} from x=0x = 0 to 44. Each is a height times a width; together they nearly fill the region. With 8 of them the sum is 10.375; with 64 it is 9.459; in the limit it is exactly the area, 283\tfrac{28}{3} ≈ 9.333.

This chapter: what the area under a rate graph means; estimating it with sums; the definite integral as the limit of those sums; the Fundamental Theorem, which turns areas into antiderivatives; and the ways to find antiderivatives: the basic rules, substitution and algebra, and for BC, parts, partial fractions and improper integrals.

6.1 · Accumulating change

rate × time, added up

When a rate is constant, the change is rate × time: the area of a rectangle under the rate graph. When the rate varies, the change is still the area under its graph.

A particle moves along a line with velocity v(t)=t(4−t)v(t) = t(4 - t) m/s for 0≤t≤50 \le t \le 5. One axis is in m/s and the other in s, so area is in metres.

stretchareameaning
0 to 4 s, above323\tfrac{32}{3} ≈ 10.6710.67 m forward
4 to 5 s, below73\tfrac{7}{3} ≈ 2.332.33 m back

Area below the axis counts as negative change. The displacement is 10.67 − 2.33 = 8.33 m; the distance travelled counts both as positive, 10.67 + 2.33 = 13.00 m.

The red dot on the number line is always at the signed area so far: it runs forward while the graph is above the axis and turns back at t=4t = 4, where vv changes sign.

rectangles and trapezoids

To estimate an area, cut [a,b][a, b] into pieces, put a rectangle or a trapezoid on each, and add their areas.

6.3 · The definite integral

the limit of the sums

More, thinner pieces give a better estimate. The definite integral is what the sums approach as the pieces shrink to nothing.

As a right sum in sigma notation, with Δx=b−an\Delta x = \tfrac{b-a}{n} and xi=a+i Δxx_i = a + i\,\Delta x:

∫abf(x) dx=lim⁡n→∞∑i=1nf(xi) Δx\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x

For a continuous ff the limit exists, and left, right and midpoint sums all reach it. Under 1+x241 + \tfrac{x^2}{4} on [0,4][0, 4]:

Δx=4n\Delta x = \tfrac4n says b−a=4b - a = 4, and xi=4inx_i = \tfrac{4i}{n} says a=0a = 0: it is ∫04(1+x24) dx\int_0^4 \big(1 + \tfrac{x^2}{4}\big)\,dx. Starting at a=1a = 1 instead, xi=1+4inx_i = 1 + \tfrac{4i}{n}, the same sum is ∫15(1+(x−1)24) dx\int_1^5 \big(1 + \tfrac{(x-1)^2}{4}\big)\,dx.

6.4 · Accumulation functions

the Fundamental Theorem, part one

Let the upper limit move and the integral becomes a function: the area so far.

The top graph is ff: a semicircle of radius 2 on [−2,2][-2, 2], then segments to (4,−2)(4, -2) and (6,0)(6, 0). Below it is g(x)=∫−2xf(t) dtg(x) = \int_{-2}^{x} f(t)\,dt.

A sliver hh further on, the area has grown by about f(x) hf(x)\,h: gg rises at rate f(x)f(x). That is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

For this ff, the half disc gives ∫−22f=2π\int_{-2}^{2} f = 2\pi and the triangle below gives ∫26f=−12(4)(2)=−4\int_{2}^{6} f = -\tfrac12(4)(2) = -4, so ∫−26f=2π−4≈2.28\int_{-2}^{6} f = 2\pi - 4 \approx 2.28 and ∫62f=4\int_{6}^{2} f = 4. Scaling and adding:

6.7 · The Fundamental Theorem, part two

areas from antiderivatives

To find an area exactly you don’t need the limit of sums: any antiderivative will do. If F′=fF' = f and ff is continuous on [a,b][a, b],

∫abf(x) dx=F(b)−F(a)\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)

Why: G(x)=∫axfG(x) = \int_a^x f is one antiderivative (part one), with G(a)=0G(a) = 0; any other is G+CG + C, and CC cancels in the difference.

∫12x2 dx=[x33]12=83−13=73≈2.333\begin{aligned}\int_1^2 x^2\,dx &= \Big[\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_1^2\\&= \frac83 - \frac13 = \frac73 \approx 2.333\end{aligned}

In the figure the red area under x2x^2 grows from x=1x = 1, and the red rise F(b)−F(a)F(b) - F(a) on the right grows with it. The curve x33+C\tfrac{x^3}{3} + C bobs up and down as CC changes; the rise never does.

Read the other way, this is the net change theorem, F(b)=F(a)+∫abF′(x) dxF(b) = F(a) + \int_a^b F'(x)\,dx: if F(1)=5F(1) = 5 and F′(x)=x2F'(x) = x^2, then F(2)=5+73=223F(2) = 5 + \tfrac73 = \tfrac{22}{3}.

6.9 · Substitution

the chain rule, run backward

The chain rule says ddxF(g(x))=f(g(x)) g′(x)\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x)) = f(g(x))\,g'(x). So when an integrand has that shape, put u=g(x)u = g(x) and du=g′(x) dxdu = g'(x)\,dx:

∫f(g(x)) g′(x) dx=∫f(u) du\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du

Change the limits too, from values of xx to values of uu. With u=x2u = x^2:

∫0π/22xcos⁡(x2) dx=∫0π/2cos⁡u du=sin⁡π2−sin⁡0=1\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx\\&= \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos u\,du\\&= \sin\tfrac\pi2 - \sin 0 = 1\end{aligned}

Why: a strip of width dxdx at xx lands at u=x2u = x^2 stretched to width du=2x dxdu = 2x\,dx. The factor 2x2x is exactly that stretch, so the blue strips match in area and the red areas fill up together.

A missing constant can be fixed, ∫xcos⁡(x2) dx=12sin⁡(x2)+C\int x\cos(x^2)\,dx = \tfrac12\sin(x^2) + C; a missing xx cannot.

6.11 · BC · Integration by parts

the product rule, run backward

The product rule (uv)′=u′v+uv′(uv)' = u'v + uv', integrated and rearranged, gives

∫u dv=uv−∫v du\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du

Choose uu to get simpler when differentiated and dvdv easy to integrate. For ∫01xex dx\int_0^1 xe^x\,dx take u=xu = x, dv=exdxdv = e^x dx, so du=dxdu = dx, v=exv = e^x:

∫01xex dx=[xex]01−∫01ex dx=e−(e−1)=1\begin{aligned}\int_0^1 x e^x\,dx &= \big[x e^x\big]_0^1 - \int_0^1 e^x\,dx\\&= e - (e - 1) = 1\end{aligned}

The figure plots (u,v)=(x,ex)(u, v) = (x, e^x). The curve splits the rectangle uvuv into ∫v du\int v\,du below (red) and ∫u dv\int u\,dv beside it (blue): at x=1x = 1, (e−1)+1=e(e - 1) + 1 = e.

6.12 · BC · Partial fractions

split, then take logs

If the bottom factors into distinct linear factors, the function splits into simple fractions, each integrating to a log.

3x(x+3)=Ax+Bx+33=A(x+3)+Bx\begin{aligned}\frac{3}{x(x+3)} &= \frac{A}{x} + \frac{B}{x+3}\\3 &= A(x+3) + Bx\end{aligned}

Choose xx to kill a term: x=0x = 0 gives 3=3A3 = 3A, so A=1A = 1; x=−3x = -3 gives 3=−3B3 = -3B, so B=−1B = -1.

∫13(1x−1x+3)dx=[ln⁡xx+3]13=ln⁡12−ln⁡14=ln⁡2≈0.693\begin{aligned}&\int_1^3 \Big(\frac1x - \frac{1}{x+3}\Big)dx\\&= \Big[\ln\frac{x}{x+3}\Big]_1^3 = \ln\frac12 - \ln\frac14\\&= \ln 2 \approx 0.693\end{aligned}

In the figure the red 1x\tfrac1x and the blue −1x+3-\tfrac{1}{x+3} add, height by height, to the black curve: the red bar rises, the blue bar comes back down, and they end on it. The grey area is ln⁡2\ln 2. (If the top’s degree is not less than the bottom’s, divide first.)

6.13 · BC · Improper integrals

an endless region, a finite area?

An integral over an infinite interval, or of a function that blows up, is defined as a limit. If the limit exists the integral converges; if not, it diverges.

∫1∞dxx2=lim⁡b→∞(1−1b)=1∫1∞dxx=lim⁡b→∞ln⁡b=∞\begin{aligned}\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^2} &= \lim_{b\to\infty}\Big(1 - \frac1b\Big) = 1\\\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x} &= \lim_{b\to\infty} \ln b = \infty\end{aligned}

The two curves look alike, but in the lower figure the red area so far levels off at 1 while the blue keeps climbing. At b=8b = 8 they are 0.875 and 2.079; ln⁡b\ln b passes any bound, just slowly. In general ∫1∞x−p dx\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx converges exactly when p>1p > 1.

When the function is unbounded at an endpoint, take the limit there:

∫01dxx=lim⁡a→0+(2−2a)=2\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{\sqrt x} = \lim_{a\to 0^+}\big(2 - 2\sqrt a\big) = 2
