Chapter 4 · Derivatives in context

rates that mean something

A derivative is a rate: how fast one quantity changes as another one does. In a real setting it has units and a sign, and it answers questions.

Water pours into this cone at a steady 1.5 m³ per minute, yet the level climbs more and more slowly. At a depth of 3 m it rises 0.212 m per minute; the derivative says exactly how fast, and why it slows.

This chapter reads derivatives as the velocity of a moving particle and as rates of cooling, growing and filling. It links rates that change together (related rates), uses the tangent line to estimate values (linearization), and uses derivatives to find limits of the forms 0/0 and ∞/∞ (L’Hôpital’s rule).

4.1 · The derivative in context

units and sign

If y = f(x), then f ′(x) is the rate of change of y with respect to x. Its units are units of y per unit of x; its sign says whether y is rising or falling.

Coffee poured at 90 °C cools in a 20 °C room: T(t)=20+70e−0.08tT(t) = 20 + 70e^{-0.08t}, t in minutes. The slope of the red tangent line is

T′(t)=−5.6e−0.08tT'(t) = -5.6e^{-0.08t}
T′(10)=−2.52 ∘C/minT'(10) = -2.52\ {}^\circ\text{C/min}

Meaning: at t = 10 minutes, the temperature of the coffee is decreasing at 2.52 °C per minute. A full answer names the quantity, the moment, the direction and the units.

It does not say the coffee lost 2.52 °C over the first 10 minutes: that average rate is (51.45 − 90)/10 = −3.85 °C/min. And since T′′(10)=0.201 ∘C/min2>0T''(10) = 0.201\ {}^\circ\text{C/min}^2 > 0, the cooling itself is slowing.

4.2 · Position, velocity, acceleration

motion along a line

For a particle moving along a line with position x(t), the velocity is v = x′ and the acceleration is a = v′ = x″.

x(t)=t3−6t2+9tx(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t

so v = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3) and a = 6t − 12. The particle is at rest when v = 0: at t = 1 and t = 3. It changes direction only where v changes sign, which happens at both. It moves right (v > 0) on 0 < t < 1 and t > 3, and left on 1 < t < 3. The trail beside the line shows each leg in its own lane.

4.3 · Speeding up or slowing down

compare the signs of v and a

Speed is |v|. A particle is speeding up when v and a have the same sign, and slowing down when their signs are opposite.

A negative acceleration alone doesn’t mean slowing down: on 1 < t < 2 both are negative, so the particle moves left faster and faster. The shaded bands mark where it speeds up.

At t = 2, a = 0 and the leftward speed peaks at 3 m/s. On a velocity graph the rule reads at a glance: the particle speeds up wherever the graph moves away from the t-axis.

4.4 · Rates in other contexts

with respect to anything

A rate of change need not be with respect to time. The derivative of any quantity with respect to another has the units of the first per unit of the second.

A balloon’s volume depends on its radius: V=43πr3V = \tfrac43\pi r^3, in cm³ with r in cm.

dVdr=4πr2=4π(3)2=36π≈113.1\frac{dV}{dr} = 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi(3)^2 = 36\pi \approx 113.1

Meaning: when the radius is 3 cm, the volume is increasing at about 113.1 cm³ per centimetre of radius.

4πr² is the balloon’s surface area, and the red shell shows why: a thin layer of thickness Δr holds about (area)·Δr. For Δr = 0.25 that is 28.27 cm³; the true gain is 30.70 cm³.

Economics reads the same way: if C(x) is the cost in dollars of making x items, C′(x) in dollars per item is about the cost of one more.

4.5 · Rates from a table

estimating a derivative from data

When a quantity is known only at a few times, estimate its derivative with a difference quotient over the closest interval that contains the point.

t (hours)025912
W(t) (gallons)120160205236248

To estimate W ′(7), use t = 5 and t = 9, which bracket it:

W′(7)≈W(9)−W(5)9−5=314W'(7) \approx \frac{W(9) - W(5)}{9 - 5} = \frac{31}{4}

that is, 7.75 gal/h.

Meaning: at t = 7 hours the amount of water is increasing at about 7.75 gallons per hour. The secant’s slope is the average rate over [5, 9], our best stand-in for the instantaneous one.

Over all 12 hours the average rate is (248 − 120)/12 = 10.67 gal/h. The slopes fall interval by interval (20, 15, 7.75, 4), so the tank is filling ever more slowly.

4.6 · Related rates

quantities that change together

When two quantities are tied by an equation and both change with time, differentiating the equation with respect to t ties their rates together.

A stone drops into a pond and the ripple’s radius grows at 0.5 m/s. The area inside is A = πr². Both A and r are functions of t, so the chain rule gives

dAdt=2πr drdt\frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r\,\frac{dr}{dt}

When r = 4 m, dAdt=2π(4)(0.5)=4π≈12.57 m2/s\frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi(4)(0.5) = 4\pi \approx 12.57\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}.

The radius grows steadily, but the area grows faster and faster: each moment it gains a strip as long as the circumference, 2πr (the dark red ring).

Substitute r = 4 only after differentiating. Put it in first and A = 16π is a constant, whose derivative is 0.

4.7 · The sliding ladder

solving a related-rates problem

The method: draw it, name the changing quantities, write the equation that links them, differentiate with respect to t, and only then substitute the moment’s values.

A 5 m ladder leans on a wall. Its foot is pulled out at 0.4 m/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the foot is 3 m from the wall?

x2+y2=25x^2 + y^2 = 25
2xdxdt+2ydydt=02x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0

so dy/dt = −(x/y)(dx/dt). At x = 3, y = √(25 − 9) = 4:

dydt=−34(0.4)=−0.3 m/s\frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{3}{4}(0.4) = -0.3\ \text{m/s}

The top is sliding down at 0.3 m/s; the minus sign is the “down”. The foot moves steadily, yet the top speeds up as it falls, because x/y grows: at x = 4.6 it is already 0.94 m/s.

4.8 · The filling cone

eliminate the extra variable

When the equation has one variable too many, find another relation (often similar triangles) and use it to remove the variable whose rate you don’t know.

An inverted cone 2 m in radius and 4 m deep fills at 1.5 m³/min. How fast does the level rise when the water is 3 m deep?

The water is a cone too: V=13πr2hV = \tfrac13\pi r^2h. By similar triangles r/h = 2/4, so r = h/2 and

V=πh312 ⇒ dVdt=πh24dhdtV = \frac{\pi h^3}{12} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi h^2}{4}\frac{dh}{dt}

At h = 3: 1.5=9π4dhdt1.5 = \frac{9\pi}{4}\frac{dh}{dt}, so

dhdt=69π≈0.212 m/min\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{6}{9\pi} \approx 0.212\ \text{m/min}

πh²/4 is the area of the water’s surface, πr². The same inflow spreads over a wider and wider surface, so the level rises ever more slowly.

4.9 · The lengthening shadow

similar triangles again

Light travels in straight lines, so a lamp, a person and the tip of their shadow make similar triangles.

A 1.8 m person walks away from a 6 m street lamp at 1.2 m/s. Let x be their distance from the post and s the length of their shadow. The big triangle (lamp) and the small one (person) share the angle at the tip:

s1.8=x+s6\frac{s}{1.8} = \frac{x+s}{6}

so 6s = 1.8x + 1.8s, 4.2s = 1.8x and s = (3/7)x. Differentiate:

dsdt=37dxdt=37(1.2)≈0.514 m/s\frac{ds}{dt} = \tfrac37\frac{dx}{dt} = \tfrac37(1.2) \approx 0.514\ \text{m/s}

The shadow lengthens at 0.514 m/s, wherever the walker is: the rate doesn’t depend on x. The tip moves at d(x+s)dt=107(1.2)≈1.714\frac{d(x+s)}{dt} = \tfrac{10}{7}(1.2) \approx 1.714 m/s, faster than the walker. Read the question carefully: “the shadow’s length” and “the shadow’s tip” are different rates.

4.10 · Local linearity

the tangent line as an estimate

Zoom in far enough on a differentiable function and its graph looks straight: it is nearly its own tangent line.

So near x = a, the tangent line, or linearization, approximates the function:

L(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)

For f(x)=xf(x) = \sqrt{x} at a = 4: f(4) = 2 and f′(x)=12xf'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt x}, so f ′(4) = 1/4 and

4.2≈L(4.2)=2+14(0.2)=2.05\sqrt{4.2} \approx L(4.2) = 2 + \tfrac14(0.2) = 2.05

The true value is 2.04939: off by 0.00061. The figure magnifies the window around (4, 2) a hundredfold; the curve straightens onto the red line. Far from a the estimate gets worse, so use a point you know close to the one you want.

4.11 · Over or under?

concavity decides

Whether a tangent-line estimate is too big or too small depends on which side of the curve the tangent line lies, and that is concavity.

If f ″ < 0 near a (concave down), the curve bends below its tangent: L(x) is an overestimate. If f ″ > 0 (concave up), it bends above: an underestimate.

For √x, f′′(x)=−14x−3/2<0f''(x) = -\tfrac14 x^{-3/2} < 0, so 4.2≈2.05\sqrt{4.2} \approx 2.05 is an overestimate (the red gap).

For ex at 0 the tangent line is L(x) = 1 + x, and (ex)′′=ex>0(e^x)'' = e^x > 0, so

e−0.2≈1+(−0.2)=0.8e^{-0.2} \approx 1 + (-0.2) = 0.8

is an underestimate: the true value is 0.8187 (the blue gap). On the AP exam, justify with the sign of f ″ on the interval between a and x.

4.12 · L’Hôpital’s rule

the form 0/0

If f(x) and g(x) both approach 0 (or both approach ±∞) as x → a, then the limit of f/g equals the limit of f ′/g ′, if that exists.

lim⁡x→af(x)g(x)=lim⁡x→af′(x)g′(x)\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}

At x = 1, ln x/(x² − 1) is 0/0. Differentiate top and bottom separately (this is not the quotient rule):

lim⁡x→1ln⁡xx2−1=lim⁡x→11/x2x=12\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\ln x}{x^2-1} = \lim_{x\to1}\frac{1/x}{2x} = \frac12

Why it works: near a both functions are nearly their tangent lines, f ≈ f ′(a)(x − a) and g ≈ g ′(a)(x − a), and the (x − a) cancels. In the figure both heights shrink to 0 while their ratio settles at the ratio of the slopes, 1 to 2.

Check the form first: if it is not 0/0 or ∞/∞, the rule does not apply.

4.13 · L’Hôpital at infinity

the form ∞/∞, used twice

The rule works the same for ∞/∞, and it may be used again as long as the result is still indeterminate.

lim⁡x→∞x2ex=lim⁡x→∞2xex=lim⁡x→∞2ex=0\begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2}{e^x} &= \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x}{e^x} \\ &= \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2}{e^x} = 0\end{aligned}

Both steps start from ∞/∞. Each changes the function but not the limit: the three curves differ, yet all fall to 0 together.
