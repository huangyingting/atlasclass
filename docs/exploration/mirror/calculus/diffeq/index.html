Chapter 7 · Differential equations

how a rate decides a function

A differential equation states how fast a quantity changes in terms of the quantity itself. Solving it means finding the function that changes that way.

The figure is the equation for a population of fish in a lake:

dPdt=0.6 P(1−P1200)\frac{dP}{dt} = 0.6\,P\left(1 - \frac{P}{1200}\right)

At every point (t, P) it gives a slope, drawn as a short segment. Every solution follows the segments. Lakes stocked with 40, 150, 450 or 1550 fish all end at the same size, 1200, without anyone solving anything.

The chapter writes such equations from words (7.1), checks proposed solutions (7.2), pictures them (7.3, 7.4), steps along them (7.5), solves them exactly (7.6, 7.7), and studies the two most important models, exponential and logistic growth (7.8, 7.9).

7.1 · Modeling with differential equations

a sentence about a rate, as an equation

“The coffee cools at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the room.” That sentence is a differential equation.

The rate is dT/dt; how much hotter is T − 20; proportional means a constant multiple, negative because hot coffee cools:

dTdt=−k (T−20)\frac{dT}{dt} = -k\,(T - 20)

With k = 0.1 per minute it gives the rate at any temperature, without knowing T(t). The red tangent’s slope is always −0.1 times the blue gap.

Likewise “proportional to √y” is dy/dt = k√y, and “jointly to y and 100 − y” is ky(100 − y).

7.2 · Verifying solutions

substitute, and both sides must agree

A function is a solution if putting it into the equation makes the two sides equal for every x in an interval. One point is not enough.

Test y = x − 1 + 2e−x in dy/dx = x − y:

dydx=1−2e−xx−y=x−(x−1+2e−x)=1−2e−x\begin{aligned} \frac{dy}{dx} &= 1 - 2e^{-x} \\ x - y &= x - (x - 1 + 2e^{-x}) \\ &= 1 - 2e^{-x} \end{aligned}

They match, so it is a solution, and y(0) = 1 picks it out. Now y = x + 2e−x: its derivative is the same, 1 − 2e−x, but x − y = −2e−x. They differ by 1 everywhere: not a solution.

In the figure each curve carries its tangent (red, the slope it has) and a segment of slope x − y (blue, the slope the equation demands). On the solution they lie on top of each other.

Second derivatives work the same way: y = sin 2x gives y″ = −4 sin 2x = −4y, so it solves y″ = −4y.

7.3 · Sketching slope fields

the equation, drawn point by point

A differential equation gives a slope at every point. Draw a short segment with that slope at each point of a grid: that is a slope field.

For dy/dx = x − y, evaluate at each point:

(x, y)(0, 0)(1, 0)(0, 1)(2, −1)(−1, 2)
slope01−13−3

Shortcuts save work. The slope is the same all along any line x − y = c: on y = x every segment is flat (dashed line); one step to the right of it, on y = x − 1, every slope is 1. Moving right raises the slope, moving up lowers it, so the top left is steeply downhill and the bottom right steeply uphill.

If the equation involves only x, every column of segments is parallel; if only y, every row is.

7.4 · Reasoning using slope fields

following the segments

A solution curve is tangent to the field everywhere it goes. Start at a point and follow the segments.

From (0, 1) the slope is −1, so the curve falls. It levels off where it meets y = x, at (ln 2, ln 2) ≈ (0.69, 0.69), its lowest point, then climbs.

Concavity comes from the equation too. Differentiate both sides with respect to x, remembering that y depends on x:

d2ydx2=1−dydx=1−x+y\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 1 - \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 - x + y

At (0, 1) that is 2 > 0: concave up. It is positive at every point above the line y = x − 1, so every solution above that line is concave up, and every one below it concave down.

The line itself (blue) is a solution, and as x grows every other solution, y = x − 1 + Ce−x, closes in on it.

7.5 · BC · Euler’s method

stepping along tangent lines

When a solution can’t be found exactly, step along it. From a known point, move a short distance h along the tangent, then work out the slope again where you land. With xn+1 = xn + h:

yn+1=yn+h⋅f(xn,yn)y_{n+1} = y_n + h \cdot f(x_n, y_n)

7.6 · Separation of variables

the general solution

If dy/dx is a function of x times a function of y, gather every y with dy on one side, every x with dx on the other, and integrate both sides.

Solve dy/dx = y cos x:

∫dyy=∫cos⁡x dxln⁡∣y∣=sin⁡x+Cy=Aesin⁡x\begin{aligned} \int \frac{dy}{y} &= \int \cos x\,dx \\ \ln|y| &= \sin x + C \\ y &= Ae^{\sin x} \end{aligned}

Only one constant is needed: constants from both sides combine into one. Exponentiating gives |y| = eCesin x, and the ± and eC merge into one constant A.

Dividing by y assumed y ≠ 0, so check y = 0 separately: it is a solution too (A = 0).

Check by differentiating: Aesin x · cos x = y cos x. This is the general solution: a family of curves, one for each A. Here A = y(0), because sin 0 = 0.

7.7 · Particular solutions

one curve, and where it lives

An initial condition picks one curve from the family. Find the constant as soon as you have integrated, before solving for y.

Solve dy/dx = 2xy² with y(0) = 1:

∫y−2 dy=∫2x dx−1y=x2+C\begin{aligned} \int y^{-2}\,dy &= \int 2x\,dx \\ -\frac{1}{y} &= x^2 + C \end{aligned}

At x = 0, y = 1: −1 = 0 + C, so C = -1. Then −1/y = x² − 1, and

y=11−x2,−1<x<1y = \frac{1}{1 - x^2}, \qquad -1 < x < 1

The domain matters. A solution must be differentiable on an interval containing the initial point. The formula breaks at x = ±1, so the particular solution is only the middle piece. The grey pieces satisfy the equation too, but the asymptotes cut them off from (0, 1).

7.8 · Exponential models

dy/dt = ky

When a quantity changes at a rate proportional to itself, the solution is always exponential:

dydt=ky⇒y=y0 ekt\frac{dy}{dt} = ky \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = y_0\,e^{kt}

(Separate: ∫ dy/y = ∫ k dt, so ln|y| = kt + C.) k > 0 is growth, k < 0 decay. A culture of 500 bacteria doubles every 4 hours:

e4k=2 ⇒ k=ln⁡24≈0.1733e^{4k} = 2 \;\Rightarrow\; k = \frac{\ln 2}{4} \approx 0.1733

After 10 hours, P = 500e10k = 500 · 22.5 ≈ 2828. When P = 2000, it is growing at kP = 346.6 bacteria per hour: the bigger it is, the faster it grows.

The coffee of 7.1 fits too: its excess D = T − 20 obeys dD/dt = −0.1D, so T = 20 + 70e−0.1t, 45.8 °C after 10 minutes.

7.9 · BC · Logistic models

growth with a ceiling

Real populations can’t grow exponentially forever. The logistic equation slows growth as P nears a carrying capacity L:

dPdt=kP(1−PL)\frac{dP}{dt} = kP\left(1 - \frac{P}{L}\right)

Read it without solving. For small P it is nearly kP: exponential. As P → L the rate → 0. Above L the rate is negative, so P falls back. P = 0 and P = L are constant solutions, and for any P(0) > 0, P → L.

As a function of P the rate is a parabola (below), highest halfway between its zeros. So growth is fastest at P = L/2, where P(t) has its inflection point.

A lake holds at most L = 1200 fish; k = 0.6 per year; it is stocked with 100. The fastest growth is at 600 fish, at 0.60 · 600(1 − ½) = 180 fish per year. With the solution P = 1200/(1 + 11e−0.6t), that happens at t = ln 11/0.6 ≈ 4.00 years.
