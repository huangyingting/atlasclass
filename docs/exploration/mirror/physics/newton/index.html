Chapter 2 · Newton’s laws

what changes motion

Kinematics described motion. Dynamics explains it: a force is a push or a pull, and forces are what change motion.

A force is a vector, drawn as an arrow from the object’s centre. 1 newton is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². This 2.0 kg block feels two:

2.1 · Tension

a string can only pull

Tension T is the pull of a string or rope, along the string. A string can pull, never push.

On the left the 2.0 kg block hangs from one string. It is in equilibrium, so T = mg = 19.6 N.

On the right two strings share the load. Only their vertical parts hold the block up, so 2T sin θ = mg and

T=mg2sin⁡θT = \frac{mg}{2\sin\theta}

As the strings flatten, θ shrinks and the tension grows: 10.4 N at 70°, 19.6 N at 30°, 28.7 N at 20°. That is why a washing line can never be pulled perfectly straight. The horizontal parts cancel each other.

2.2 · The first law

inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object moving in a straight line keeps moving at the same velocity, unless a nonzero net force acts on it.

This block slides across a frictionless surface at 1.5 m/s. Its weight and the normal force cancel, so ΣF = 0 and nothing changes its motion. The strobe images are evenly spaced.

Equilibrium means ΣF = 0. That includes moving at constant velocity, not just being at rest. Nothing is needed to keep it moving.

2.3 · Level flight

moving, but in equilibrium

A 1200 kg plane cruising at a steady 55 m/s, level, in a straight line. Its velocity isn’t changing, so by the first law the net force is zero.

lift (up, from the wings)11.76 kN
weight (down)11.76 kN
thrust (forward, from the propeller)1.80 kN
drag (back, from the air)1.80 kN

Drag is the force a fluid exerts against motion through it. It grows with speed, roughly as Fd=12DρAv2F_\text{d} = \tfrac12 D\rho Av^2 (D the drag coefficient, A the area facing the flow). The engine doesn’t need to keep the plane moving; it needs to cancel the drag.

2.4 · The second law

ΣF = ma

A net force causes an acceleration in its direction, of size ΣFm\frac{\Sigma F}{m}.

Both blocks get the same 6.0 N push on a frictionless surface. The 2.0 kg block accelerates at 3.0 m/s². The 4.0 kg block, with twice the mass, gets half: 1.5 m/s².

More force, more acceleration. More mass, less acceleration.

2.5 · What pushes a car?

the road does

The engine turns the wheels, but the engine is part of the car, and nothing can push itself. The forward force on the car comes from the road: static friction on the tyres.

The tyres push back on the road. By the third law the road pushes the tyres forward, here with 3.6 kN. The tyre doesn’t slip, so this is static friction, even though the car moves.

Air drag pushes back and grows with v²: just 36 N at 9.6 m/s, but 351 N at highway speed (30 m/s). The net force, and so the acceleration, shrinks as the car speeds up.

Arrows: 25 cm per kilonewton.

2.6 · Friction

static, then kinetic

On a rough floor the push grows steadily, 4.0 N every second. At first the block doesn’t move: static friction matches the push exactly.

Static friction has a limit, μsFN = 0.50 × 19.6 = 9.80 N. Push harder and the block breaks free. Kinetic friction then takes over at a smaller, steady μkFN = 5.88 N, whatever the push. The red arrow, the net force, appears only once the push wins.

2.7 · The third law

forces come in pairs

If A pushes on B, B pushes on A with a force of the same size in the opposite direction, at the same instant.

A 6.0 N push moves both blocks together at 1.0 m/s². To give B (4.0 kg) that acceleration, A must push it with 4.0 N. B pushes A back with 4.0 N, so the net force on A is 2.0 N, which is exactly 2.0 kg × 1.0 m/s².

The two forces of a pair act on different objects, so they never cancel each other. Weight and normal force are not a pair: both act on the same block. The partner of the block’s weight is the block pulling the Earth up.

2.8 · Choosing a system

internal forces cancel

Draw a boundary around any objects you like and call them the system. Forces between things inside it are internal. Forces from anything outside are external.

Skater A (50 kg) and skater B (75 kg) stand on frictionless ice and haul in a light rope. The rope pulls each of them with 60 N, so A accelerates at 1.2 m/s² and B at 0.8 m/s², toward each other.

Take skaters + rope as the system. The tensions are internal, and they come in equal and opposite pairs, so they add to zero. Weight and the normal force are external, but they cancel. The system as a whole can’t accelerate. Its centre of mass stays put:

2.9 · The centre of mass accelerates

ΣF_ext = M a_cm

Write F = ma for every piece of a system and add them up. The internal forces cancel in pairs, and what is left is the second law for the whole system:

ΣFext=Macm,M=Σm\Sigma F_\text{ext} = M a_\text{cm}, \quad M = \Sigma m

A 2.0 kg block rests on a 4.0 kg board on ice, and a rope pulls the board with 18 N. That is more than static friction can share out (μsMg = 11.76 N), so the block slips. Kinetic friction, 2.94 N, drags the block forward and the board back.

Check: 2.0×1.47+4.0×3.776.0=3.00 m/s2\frac{2.0 \times 1.47 + 4.0 \times 3.77}{6.0} = 3.00\ \text{m/s}^2. The friction never mattered to acm. Yank a tablecloth from under the dishes and the same thing happens: friction is all that drags the dishes, and it can give them only μg.

2.10 · The inclined plane

tilt the axes

On a slope, measure along it and perpendicular to it. Then the weight splits into two parts.

along the slopemgsin⁡θ=9.80 Nmg\sin\theta = 9.80\ \text{N}
into the slopemgcos⁡θ=16.97 Nmg\cos\theta = 16.97\ \text{N}

Nothing accelerates into the slope, so the normal force cancels the second part: FN = mg cos θ = 16.97 N. Only the part along the slope is left: a = g sin θ = 4.90 m/s².

Add friction and the block slides at constant speed when tan θ = μk. For μk = 0.30 that is 16.7°.

2.11 · AP Physics C · Drag proportional to v

terminal velocity

Drag points against the velocity and grows with speed. For small, slow objects in thick fluids it is simply proportional to speed: Fd = −bv.

A 0.50 kg ball with b = 1.25 kg/s is let go from rest. Take down as positive:

mdvdt=mg−bvm\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv

At first v = 0 and a = g. Drag grows as it speeds up, so the net force shrinks. When bv = mg it is zero: the terminal velocity vt=mgb=3.92 m/sv_\text{t} = \frac{mg}{b} = 3.92\ \text{m/s}. Separate the variables, with τ=mb\tau = \frac{m}{b}:

dvvt−v=dtτ\frac{dv}{v_\text{t} - v} = \frac{dt}{\tau}
∫0vdv′vt−v′=∫0tdt′τ\int_0^v \frac{dv'}{v_\text{t} - v'} = \int_0^t \frac{dt'}{\tau}
−ln⁡(1−vvt)=tτ-\ln\left(1 - \frac{v}{v_\text{t}}\right) = \frac{t}{\tau}
v(t)=vt(1−e−t/τ)v(t) = v_\text{t}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)

Here τ = 0.40 s. After one τ the ball has 1−1e1 - \frac{1}{e} = 63.2% of vt; after 3τ, 95%; after 5τ, 99.3%. It never quite gets there.

2.12 · AP Physics C · Real fluids

position, and which drag law

Integrate once more for the position fallen:

x(t)=∫0tv dt′=vt[t−τ(1−e−t/τ)]x(t) = \int_0^t v\,dt' = v_\text{t}\left[t - \tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\right]

After a few τ this is vt(t − τ): the ball falls as if it had been at vt all along but started τ late (dashed below).

Linear drag holds for small, slow objects in thick fluids. Big or fast ones push fluid aside, and drag grows as kv², like the car’s on page 2.5. Then vt=mg/kv_\text{t} = \sqrt{mg/k}.
