Chapter 10 · Electric flux

how much field passes through

The flux Φ of a field through a surface measures how much of the field passes through it. Count the field lines that pierce the surface.

Φ=E⃗⋅A⃗=EAcos⁡θ\Phi = \vec{E}\cdot\vec{A} = EA\cos\theta

The area vector A is perpendicular to the surface, as long as its area; θ is the angle between E and A. Bigger field, bigger area, or more face-on: more flux. Units: N·m²/C.

This 20 cm square turns in a uniform 2.0 kN/C field. Face on, Φ = 80 N·m²/C; edge on, 0; turned past 90°, negative. Through any closed surface a uniform field’s flux is 0.

10.1 · Gauss’s law

flux out = charge inside

Through any closed surface, the total flux out is the charge enclosed divided by ε0.

∮E⃗⋅dA⃗=qencε0\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_\text{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}

dA points outward. The sphere breathes, but Φ stays 113 N·m²/C: E falls as 1/r21/r^2, the area 4πr² grows as r². On it E ∥ dA and E is constant, so

E⋅4πr2=qε0⇒E=kqr2E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = \frac{kq}{r^2}

which is Coulomb’s law. The box holds no charge: every line that enters it leaves, so its flux is zero.

Click the sphere for dA, the box for each face. Arrows: 1.0 square per kN/C. One grid square is 10 cm.

10.2 · A line of charge

a cylinder around it

An infinite line with charge λ per metre. By symmetry E points straight out from the line, so wrap it in a coaxial cylinder of radius r and length L.

Split the surface into two caps and a curved side. On the caps E ⊥ dA: no flux. On the side E ∥ dA and E is constant:

E⋅2πrL=λLε0⇒E=2kλrE \cdot 2\pi rL = \frac{\lambda L}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}

L cancels, and the field falls as 1/r1/r, slower than a point charge’s. Here λ = 2.00 nC/m, so E = 360 N/C at 10 cm.

Click the cylinder to take its caps off. Arrows: 1.5 squares per kN/C. One grid square is 10 cm.

10.4 · A metal sphere

inside and out

On a conductor, the charge spreads to the surface. A Gaussian sphere inside the metal encloses none of it.

r>Rr > Rqenc=Qq_\text{enc} = QE⋅4πr2=Q/ε0E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = Q/\varepsilon_0 → E=kQ/r2E = kQ/r^2

10.5 · Insulating sphere

charge all the way through

In an insulator charge stays put: here spread evenly through the volume, ρ=Q/(43πR3)\rho = Q/(\tfrac43 \pi R^3) = 0.477 μC/m³.

Inside, qenc = ρ⁴⁄₃πr³: use small r inside, big R outside.

E⋅4πr2=ρ43πr3/ε0E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = \rho\tfrac43 \pi r^3/\varepsilon_0E=ρr/3ε0E = \rho r/3\varepsilon_0, inside
E⋅4πr2=ρ43πR3/ε0E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = \rho\tfrac43 \pi R^3/\varepsilon_0E=kQ/r2E = kQ/r^2, outside

E grows linearly to a peak at the surface, then falls as 1/r21/r^2. Outside, it matches the metal sphere (same Q, R). If hollow, E = 0 in the hollow.

10.8 · Conductors

in equilibrium: the Faraday cage

Once the charges in a conductor stop moving:

  1. E = 0 everywhere inside the metal.
  2. Any excess charge sits on the surface.
  3. Just outside, E is perpendicular to the surface, with E=σ/ε0E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0.
  4. A hollow inside, with no charge in it, has no field: a Faraday cage.

This uncharged shell sits in a uniform 1.0 kN/C field. Electrons crowd to the left, leaving the right positive; their field cancels E0 inside and bends the lines to meet the surface square-on. (With a current flowing, E in a wire is not zero.)

Click the shell for a Gaussian surface in the metal. Arrows: 0.4 squares per kN/C. One grid square is 10 cm.
