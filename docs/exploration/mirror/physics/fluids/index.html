Chapter 8 · Fluids

density

A fluid is anything that flows: a liquid or a gas. Its molecules are not locked in place, so it takes the shape of its container. We describe it by its density, mass per volume:

ρ=mV\rho = \frac{m}{V}

These five cubes are all 10 cm on a side, 1.0 L each: the same volume, very different masses, from 0.24 kg of cork to 7.87 kg of iron. On a graph of mass against volume each material is a straight line whose slope is its density.

Density belongs to the material, not the object: cut a cube in half and both m and V halve. An ideal fluid is incompressible (its density never changes) and flows without friction. Water is very nearly one.

8.1 · Pressure

force spread over an area

Pressure is the force pushing straight onto a surface, per area:

P=F⊥AP = \frac{F_\perp}{A}

It is a scalar, measured in pascals: 1 Pa = 1 N/m². The same 70 kg person (686 N) stands on snow twice:

areapressure
shoes0.050 m²13.7 kPa
snowshoes0.40 m²1.72 kPa

Eight times the area, one eighth of the pressure, so the snow holds. A nail works the other way: push its 1 cm² head with 50 N and its 0.5 mm² tip presses on the wood at 100 MPa.

8.2 · Pressure and depth

the weight of the water above

Under water, pressure comes from the weight of the water above. A column of height h and area A weighs ρAh g, so it adds ρgh to the pressure on top:

P=P0+ρghP = P_0 + \rho g h

P0 = 101.3 kPa is the atmosphere pressing on the surface. P is the absolute pressure; ρgh alone is the gauge pressure, the part above atmospheric. Every 10.3 m of water adds about one more atmosphere: at 10 m the diver feels 199 kPa.

At one depth the pressure is the same in every direction, whatever the shape of the water around.

Arrows 1.2 cm per kPa. Click the diver to see the column of water above.

8.3 · Pascal’s principle

a push felt everywhere

Push on a liquid shut in a container and the extra pressure reaches every part of it, and every wall, undiminished. That is Pascal’s principle.

A 50 N push on the small piston adds ΔP=F1A1\Delta P = \frac{F_1}{A_1} = 2.49 kPa everywhere. Under the large piston, 16 times the area, the same ΔP gives 800 N, enough to hold up 81.6 kg:

F1A1=F2A2\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}

Nothing is free. Water can’t be squeezed, so the volume pushed down on the left comes up on the right: A1d1 = A2d2. The big piston moves 16 times less, so the work in equals the work out, F1d1 = F2d2.

8.4 · Buoyancy

Archimedes’ principle

Water pushes on every face of a submerged object, and it pushes harder lower down. The sideways pushes cancel; the push up on the bottom beats the push down on the top. The difference is the buoyant force.

For a cube of side s: (Pbottom − Ptop)s² = ρgs·s² = ρVg, the weight of the water it pushes aside:

Fb=ρfluidVdisplaced gF_b = \rho_\text{fluid} V_\text{displaced}\, g

It grows as the cube goes in, then stays at 265 N however deep. The string holds the rest, the apparent weight T = W − Fb = 450 N instead of 714 N.

Gauge pressure, 2.0 cm per kPa; forces 0.6 mm per N. Click for each face’s total push.

8.5 · Float or sink

compare densities

If an object is less dense than water it sinks only until the water it displaces weighs as much as it does: ρfluidVunderg = ρobjVg, so

VunderV=ρobjρfluid\frac{V_\text{under}}{V} = \frac{\rho_\text{obj}}{\rho_\text{fluid}}

8.6 · Continuity

what goes in comes out

Water can’t be squeezed and the pipe doesn’t leak, so every second the same volume passes every cross-section. That is the volume flow rate, Q = Av:

A1v1=A2v2A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2

The pipe narrows to half the radius, a quarter of the area, so the water speeds up four times: from 0.5 m/s to 2.0 m/s. Q = 141 L/s everywhere.

Follow the dark slice of water: in the wide part it is short and fat; in the narrow part the same volume is long and thin. A thumb over a hose does the same thing.

8.7 · Bernoulli

energy conservation for a fluid

Follow a bit of water along the pipe. Where it speeds up, something has to push it forward: the pressure behind it must be higher than ahead. So fast flow means low pressure.

8.8 · Torricelli’s law

a jet from a hole

Water leaves a hole h below the surface. Apply Bernoulli from the open surface (atmospheric, nearly still) to the jet (atmospheric again): the pressure terms cancel, leaving ρgh = ½ρv², so

v=2ghv = \sqrt{2gh}

The same speed as a drop falling freely through h. Here h starts at 1.2 m, so v = 4.85 m/s. As the tank drains, h and v fall together.

Once out, every drop is a projectile: v across, falling 1.08 m in 0.469 s. Its range is vt=2h yholevt = 2\sqrt{h\,y_\text{hole}}, 2.28 m when full.
