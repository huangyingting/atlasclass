Magnetic Fields

Chapter 14 · Magnetic fields

made by moving charge

A magnetic field B is made by moving charge: a current, or the spinning electrons inside a magnet. It is measured in tesla (1 T = 104 gauss).

Outside a magnet the lines run from N to S; inside, from S back to N, so every line closes on itself. There are no magnetic monopoles (cut one and you get two magnets), so through any closed surface

∮B⃗⋅dA⃗=0\oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A} = 0

This 20 cm magnet is drawn as Ampère pictured it, a sheet of current round its sides. Every line is traced from that current’s Biot–Savart sum.

Click the magnet for a closed box round its N end. One grid square is 10 cm.

14.1 · The magnetic force

F = qv × B

A magnetic field pushes only on a moving charge, and sideways: at right angles to both v and B.

F⃗=q v⃗×B⃗,F=qvBsin⁡θ\vec{F} = q\,\vec{v}\times\vec{B},\quad F = qvB\sin\theta

Right-hand rule: thumb along v, index finger along B; the middle finger gives F on a + charge, reversed for a − charge. Moving along B, there is no force.

F ⊥ v always, so B does no work: it turns a charge but never speeds it up. With E too, F = qE + qv × B (the Lorentz force).

Click to split v along and across B. v = 2.0×10⁵ m/s, B = 0.50 T; F is 1.5 squares at 1.6×10⁻¹⁴ N.

14.2 · Circling in a field

r = mv/qB

Across a uniform B the force stays at right angles to the velocity. It is a centripetal force: the charge circles at constant speed.

qvB=mv2r⇒r=mvqB,ω=qBmqvB = \frac{mv^2}{r}\quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{qB},\quad \omega = \frac{qB}{m}

Two protons start from rest through 50 V and 200 V (50 eV and 200 eV, with q ΔV=12mv2q\,\Delta V = \tfrac12 mv^2). Four times the energy, twice the speed, twice the radius: 1.02 and 2.04 cm.

Yet T=2πmqB=0.656 μsT = \frac{2\pi m}{qB} = 0.656\ \mu\text{s} for both, so they meet at the start every turn. A cyclotron uses this, kicking them across a gap at 1.52 MHz.

Click for the forces and radii. B = 0.10 T, down. One grid square is 1 cm; time runs 1.1×10⁷× slower.

14.3 · Crossed E and B

the velocity selector

E between the plates pushes a proton toward the − plate with qE. B into the page pushes it back with qvB. They cancel at one speed:

qE=qvB⇒v=EBqE = qvB\quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{E}{B}

Here E = 1000 V/m and B = 0.010 T, so 1.0×10⁵ m/s goes straight. Slower, qE wins and it bends down; faster, qvB wins and it bends up.

The Hall effect is the same balance in a wire: charge piles up on one edge until qE = qvdB, leaving ΔVH=vdBd=IBdnqA\Delta V_H = v_dBd = \frac{IBd}{nqA}.

14.4 · Force on a current

and the torque on a loop

A current is many moving charges. With I = nqAvd, the force on the charge nqAL in a wire becomes

F⃗=I L⃗×B⃗\vec{F} = I\,\vec{L}\times\vec{B}

On a loop in a uniform field the side forces add to zero, but the two vertical sides push opposite ways along different lines. They twist it:

τ⃗=μ⃗×B⃗,μ⃗=IA⃗\vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu}\times\vec{B},\quad \vec{\mu} = I\vec{A}

As U = −p · E for a charge dipole, U = −μ · B: lowest along B (stable), highest against it (unstable). Here μ = 0.12 A·m².

The loop is turned slowly through every angle. Click it for the force on each side (4 squares per N).

14.5 · Ampère’s law

B round a long wire

Round a long wire the lines are circles: thumb along I, fingers curl with B.

∮B⃗⋅ds⃗=μ0Ienc\oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{s} = \mu_0 I_\text{enc}

On a circle B ∥ ds, B constant: B(2πr) = μ0I, so B=μ0I2πrB = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}. Inside a thick wire, B=μ0Ir2πa2B = \frac{\mu_0 Ir}{2\pi a^2}.

Through the rectangle, add strips: ∫aa+cμ0I2πr b dr\int_a^{a+c} \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,b\,dr, or

Φ=μ0Ib2πln⁡(a+ca)\Phi = \frac{\mu_0 Ib}{2\pi}\ln\left(\frac{a + c}{a}\right)

Click the wire for ds, the rectangle for its strips. B: 0.8 squares per 100 μT.

14.6 · Biot–Savart

adding up every piece

Without symmetry, add up the field of every piece dl of wire, as Coulomb’s law adds up pieces of charge. r̂ points from dl to P:

dB⃗=μ0I4π dl⃗×r^r2d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{d\vec{l}\times\hat{r}}{r^2}

On a loop’s axis every dl ⊥ r. The sideways parts of dB cancel round the loop; the parts along the axis (cos⁡θ=Rr\cos\theta = \frac{R}{r}) add:

B=μ0IR22(R2+z2)3/2B = \frac{\mu_0 IR^2}{2(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}

At the centre, μ0I2R=41.9 μT\frac{\mu_0 I}{2R} = 41.9\ \mu\text{T}. Far away, μ0IR22z3\frac{\mu_0 IR^2}{2z^3}: a dipole’s 1z3\frac{1}{z^3}.

Click the loop for 12 pieces dl and their dB (drawn 5× longer). B: 1 square per 20 μT.

14.7 · Parallel wires

like currents attract

Each wire sits in the other’s field, so each feels F = I L × B.

B1=μ0I12πd⇒FL=μ0I1I22πdB_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi d}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}

With 20 A in each, 20 cm apart, that is 0.40 mN per metre. Currents the same way attract; opposite ways repel, the reverse of charges.

The field lines show it. Like currents cancel between the wires (B = 0 midway) and the outer lines wrap round both. Opposite currents crowd the field between them.

Click a pair for the field each wire feels from the other. Forces: 2 squares per mN/m.

14.8 · Solenoids

and toroids: a uniform field

Stack many loops: their fields add inside and nearly cancel outside. That is a solenoid.

Take a rectangle half inside. Only its inside side counts (B ⊥ ds on the ends, B ≈ 0 outside), so BL = μ0NI. With n=NLn = \frac{N}{L} turns per metre:

B=μ0nItoroid: B=μ0NI2πrB = \mu_0 nI\qquad\text{toroid: } B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2\pi r}

Here μ0nI = 251.3 μT; the Biot–Savart sum gives 240.8 μT, as this coil is only 6.7 radii long. Bent into a ring it is a toroid: B(2πr) = μ0NI inside, and 0 in the hole and outside.
