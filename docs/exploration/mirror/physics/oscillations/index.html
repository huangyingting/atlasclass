Chapter 6 · Oscillations

back and forth, and back again

An oscillation is motion back and forth about an equilibrium position. It is simple harmonic motion (SHM) when a restoring force pulls it back toward equilibrium in proportion to its displacement. Then the period never changes.

6.1 · Hooke’s law

the restoring force

Stretch or squash a spring and it pushes back, in proportion to how far its end has moved:

Fs=−kΔxF_s = -k\Delta x

k = 16 N/m is the spring constant, the stiffness. The minus sign: the force always points opposite to the displacement. Weight and normal force cancel, so Fs is the net force and a=−kmxa = -\frac{k}{m}x (red).

6.3 · Energy

traded, never lost

With no friction the total stays put. It just moves between the block’s motion and the spring:

12kA2=12mv2+12kx2\tfrac12 kA^2 = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2

At ±A it is all in the spring, 12kA2=2.00 J\tfrac12 kA^2 = 2.00\ \text{J}. At x = 0 it is all kinetic, so 12mvmax2=12kA2\tfrac12 mv_\text{max}^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2 and vmax=km⋅A=2.00 m/sv_\text{max} = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\cdot A = 2.00\ \text{m/s}. The shares are equal at x=A22=0.354 mx = \frac{A\sqrt2}{2} = 0.354\ \text{m}, not halfway.

The spring energy is the work done stretching it: the triangle under F = kx, 12(kx)x\tfrac12(kx)x.

6.4 · A hanging spring

gravity moves the middle, not the beat

Hang the same block from the same spring. It sags until the spring holds its weight, kd = mg:

d=mgk=0.613 md = \frac{mg}{k} = 0.613\ \text{m}

That is the new equilibrium. Measure y from there and gravity drops out: k(d − y) − mg = −ky. Hooke’s law again, so it bobs with the same period as on the floor, 1.571 s.

6.5 · The pendulum

SHM, if the swing is small

The string pulls along itself, so only gravity pulls the bob back along its arc:

F=−mgsin⁡θF = -mg\sin\theta

Not quite proportional to θ. But below about 10°, sin θ ≈ θ (in radians), so F ≈ −mgθ and it is SHM with

T=2πLg=2.458 sT = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} = 2.458\ \text{s}

At ±8° the true period is just 0.12% longer. At the bottom there is no restoring force and the bob is fastest: 12mv2=mgΔy\tfrac12 mv^2 = mg\Delta y gives 0.535 m/s.

6.7 · What it ignores

amplitude, and a pendulum’s mass

Amplitude does not change the period. It isn’t in either formula.

The far block starts twice as far out. Twice the force, twice the acceleration, twice the distance in the same time: the two stay in step.

Nor does a pendulum’s mass: ma = −mg sin θ, and the m cancels, as it does in free fall. The 4 kg bob keeps time with the 1 kg one.

6.8 · AP Physics C · The SHM equation

why the answer is a cosine

Put Hooke’s law into ∑F=ma\sum F = ma and you get an equation for x(t):

6.9 · AP Physics C · Physical pendulums

a rigid body on a pivot

The weight of a rigid body acts at its centre of mass, a distance d below the pivot, and twists it back:

τ=−mgdsin⁡θ≈−mgd θ\tau = -mgd\sin\theta \approx -mgd\,\theta

Iα = τ gives d2θdt2=−mgdI θ\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{mgd}{I}\,\theta: SHM, with ω=mgdI\omega = \sqrt{\frac{mgd}{I}} and

T=2πImgdT = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}}

I is about the pivot: I = Icm + md² (parallel axes).

6.10 · AP Physics C · The torsion pendulum

a twist, and a twist back

Hang a disc from a wire and turn it. The wire twists back in proportion to the angle, Hooke’s law for turning:

τ=−κθ⇒T=2πIκ\tau = -\kappa\theta \quad\Rightarrow\quad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{\kappa}}

since Iα = −κθ is the spring’s equation with m → I and k → κ, the wire’s torsion constant, 0.25 N·m/rad. The 2.0 kg disc of radius 0.30 m has I=12MR2=0.090 kg⋅m2I = \tfrac12 MR^2 = 0.090\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2, so T = 3.77 s.

No small-angle step this time: the torque stays proportional to θ however far the wire is twisted (within its limit). At 60° or at 5°, the period is the same.

6.11 · Everyday oscillators

a car, a swing

Anything with a restoring force that grows with displacement oscillates.

A car on its springs. 1200 kg on four springs, 60 kN/m together. It sags 19.6 cm under its weight; push it down and it bounces with T=2πmk=0.89 sT = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = 0.89\ \text{s}. (Real shock absorbers damp this out in a cycle or two; here they’re off.)

A swing. A child on a 2.4 m swing is a pendulum: T=2πLg=3.11 sT = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} = 3.11\ \text{s}, small pushes or big, light child or heavy. That is why you push in rhythm.
