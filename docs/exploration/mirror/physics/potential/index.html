Electric Potential

Chapter 11 · Electric potential energy

the work stored in a pair of charges

Push a charge toward another of the same sign and you do work against the repulsion. That work is stored as electric potential energy.

U=kq1q2rU = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r}

U = 0 when the charges are infinitely far apart. Bringing +2 nC in from 40 cm to 10 cm of +4 nC takes 0.539 μJ. Unlike charges pull together, so their U is negative and falls as they close. U is a scalar: the signs of the charges carry its sign.

11.2 · Electric potential

energy per unit charge

Potential V is potential energy per unit charge, in volts (J/C). A charge q placed there has U = qV.

ΔV=−∫ABE⃗⋅ds⃗\Delta V = -\int_A^B \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{s}

Only the part of each step ds along E counts. With E = 50 N/C: A → B runs across E and adds 0; B → C is 0.2 m along it, −50 × 0.2 = −10 V. A → C straight gives the same. Moving with E, V falls. The pale sheets are equipotentials, at right angles to E. For E = 4x: −∫044x dx-\int_0^4 4x\,dx = -32 V.

11.3 · The potential hill

V = kq/r, and E = −∇V

Around a point charge only the radial part of each step counts, r̂ · ds = dr:

ΔV=−∫kqr2 dr=kq(1rB−1rA)\Delta V = -\int \frac{kq}{r^2}\,dr = kq\left(\frac{1}{r_B} - \frac{1}{r_A}\right)

From infinity, V=kq/rV = kq/r, drawn as height for +1 nC. The circles are equipotentials every 30 V; they crowd in where the hill is steep. E points straight downhill, as big as the slope: E = −∇V, or E = −dV/dr-dV/dr.

11.4 · The dipole

potentials add as numbers

Potential is a scalar: for several charges, add each kq/rkq/r with its sign. No components.

Vaxis=−2akqx2−a2V_\text{axis} = -\frac{2akq}{x^2 - a^2}

That is kq/(x+a)−kq/(x−a)kq/(x + a) - kq/(x - a) for ±1 nC, 20 cm apart: a hill beside a well. At P, 20 cm out, V = −59.9 V. Every point of the perpendicular bisector is equally far from both, so V = 0 there, though E is not. For two like charges V on the bisector is not 0: it peaks at the midpoint.

11.7 · Charged spheres

conductor and insulator

Find E in each region with Gauss’s law, then walk in from infinity: ∞ → R outside, R → r inside.

Outside, both act like a point charge, V=kQ/rV = kQ/r. Inside a conductor E = 0, so V stays kQ/RkQ/R = 179.8 V. Inside an insulator E=kQr/R3E = kQr/R^3 keeps adding:

V=kQR(32−r22R2)V = \frac{kQ}{R}\left(\frac32 - \frac{r^2}{2R^2}\right)

3kQ/2R3kQ/2R = 269.7 V at the centre (+2 nC, R = 10 cm). V never jumps.

11.8 · The electron volt

energy from a potential difference

Let a charge go: potential energy turns into kinetic, and mechanical energy is conserved.

qV0=qVf+12mv2⇒v=2q ΔVmqV_0 = qV_\text{f} + \tfrac12 mv^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2q\,\Delta V}{m}}

A proton rolls “downhill” with E from 7 V to 1 V; an electron goes the other way. Each gains 6 eV (1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻19 J, one e through 1 V), but the electron is 1833 times lighter:
