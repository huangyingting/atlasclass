turning, in the language of motion

Every point on a turning wheel turns through the same angle θ (in radians) in the same time. So we describe the whole wheel with angles: ω=ΔθΔt\omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}ω=ΔtΔθ​ in rad/s, α=ΔωΔt\alpha = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta t}α=ΔtΔω​ in rad/s².

Counter-clockwise is positive. This wheel speeds up at 1.5 rad/s², then brakes at the same rate. Speeding up, ω and α have the same sign; slowing down, opposite signs. The kinematics equations carry over with x → θ, v → ω, a → α:

ω=ω0+αt\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha tω=ω0​+αtno Δθ
Δθ=ω0t+12αt2\Delta\theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2Δθ=ω0​t+21​αt2no ω
ω2=ω02+2αΔθ\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\thetaω2=ω02​+2αΔθno t

1The wheel starts from rest and speeds up at 1.5 rad/s² for 2.4 s. How many revolutions does it make in that time?

Constant α from rest: Δθ = ½αt², in radians. Then divide by 2π rad per revolution.

Δθ=12×1.5×2.42=4.32 rad=4.322π=0.688 rev\Delta\theta = \tfrac12 \times 1.5 \times 2.4^2 = 4.32\ \text{rad} = \frac{4.32}{2\pi} = 0.688\ \text{rev}Δθ=21​×1.5×2.42=4.32 rad=2π4.32​=0.688 rev

4.32 rev forgets that ½αt² comes out in radians; 1.38 rev drops the ½.

2Counter-clockwise is positive. The wheel turns counter-clockwise while it brakes. The signs of ω and α are

It turns counter-clockwise, so ω > 0. It is slowing down, so α points against ω: α < 0. The sign of ω gives the direction of turning; α only tells you which way ω is changing.

7.14 · AP Physics C

L of a particle

L = r × p

A ball sliding in a straight line has angular momentum about a point O:

L⃗=r⃗×p⃗,∣L⃗∣=mvrsin⁡ϕ=mvr⊥\vec{L} = \vec{r} \times \vec{p}, \quad |\vec{L}| = mvr\sin\phi = mvr_\perpL=r×p​,∣L∣=mvrsinϕ=mvr⊥​

r⊥ = d is the distance from O to the line of motion. As the ball slides, r and φ change but r sin φ stays d: L is constant along a straight line. Then it hits a 1.0 kg rod pinned at O. The pin has no torque about O, so L about O is conserved:

mv0d=−mvfd+13ML2ωmv_0 d = -mv_f d + \tfrac13 ML^2\omegamv0​d=−mvf​d+31​ML2ω

Elastic: the 0.3 kg ball rebounds at 0.69 m/s; the rod turns at 2.31 rad/s.

7.1 · Two riders

v = rω

On a merry-go-round both riders share one ω. The one at the edge goes round a bigger circle in the same time, so she moves faster: twice the radius, twice the speed.

Each rider also has a centripetal acceleration ac = rω² toward the middle, from the change in direction.

7.2 · Torque

what makes things turn

A push swings a door only if it acts away from the hinge and not straight at it. Its turning effect is the torque, in N·m:

τ=rFsin⁡ϕ=F×lever arm\tau = rF\sin\phi = F \times \text{lever arm}

r runs from the hinge to the push, φ is the angle between r and F. Only F sin φ, the part square to the door, turns it.

7.3 · Equilibrium

no push, no turn

A plank bridge, and a 30 kg crate slid slowly across it. To stay put, ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0.

Take torques about the point with the most unknowns: support A, where FA has no lever arm. Counter-clockwise +, clockwise −:

L⋅FB−L2⋅Mg−x⋅mg=0L \cdot F_B - \frac{L}{2} \cdot Mg - x \cdot mg = 0

Then ΣF = 0 gives FA = (M + m)g − FB. The nearer the crate is to a support, the more that support holds.

7.4 · The ladder

how low can it lean?

A 20 kg ladder on a rough floor against a smooth wall, which can only push straight out.

Forces: FN = mg and fs = Fw. Torques about the foot O, where two forces drop out:

(Lsin⁡θ)⋅Fw=(12Lcos⁡θ)⋅mg(L\sin\theta) \cdot F_w = \left(\tfrac12 L\cos\theta\right) \cdot mg

The lower the ladder, the more friction the foot needs. Friction gives at most μsmg, so it slips below tan⁡θmin=12μs\tan\theta_\text{min} = \frac{1}{2\mu_s}: 51.3° for μs = 0.40, whatever its mass or length.

I = ∫ r² dm

7.8 · Work and power

W = τΔθ, P = τω

A 50 N push on a wrench 0.40 m long turns a nut. The hand moves along an arc s = rΔθ, so the work it does is

W=F⋅s=F⋅rΔθ=τΔθW = F \cdot s = F \cdot r\Delta\theta = \tau\Delta\theta

the rotational twin of W = Fd. The rate of doing work is the power, the twin of P = Fv:

P=τωP = \tau\omega

Here τ = 20 N·m at a steady 1.5 rad/s: P = 30 W, and one 120° stroke takes 41.9 J. The thread’s friction holds ω steady, so this work becomes heat, not spin. With no friction it would go into ½Iω².

7.9 · Rolling

the race down the ramp

A sphere, a disc and a ring roll from rest down a 25.0° ramp, beside a box sliding on ice. Rolling things must spend some energy on spin:

mgh=12mv2+12Iω2mgh = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega^2

With I = fmR² and ω=vR\omega = \frac{v}{R}, v2=2gh1+fv^2 = \frac{2gh}{1 + f}. Mass and radius cancel; the smaller the shape factor f, the faster. The box wins, then sphere, disc, ring. Rolling up a hill, the spin energy buys extra height.

a bowling ball

A bowling ball leaves the hand at 2.4 m/s with no spin. Its bottom skids, so kinetic friction μkmg acts backward: it slows the ball and, acting at R, spins it up.

7.12 · Angular impulse

τΔt = ΔL

A spinning thing carries angular momentum L = Iω. A torque changes it, just as a force changes p:

Στ=ΔLΔt,soτΔt=ΔL\Sigma\tau = \frac{\Delta L}{\Delta t}, \quad \text{so} \quad \tau\Delta t = \Delta L

τΔt is the angular impulse, the area under the τ–t graph. Two equal merry-go-rounds (I = ½MR² = 108 kg·m²) get two pushes at the rim: A hard and short, B gentle and long. The areas match, so they end with the same L and the same ω = 1.33 rad/s.
